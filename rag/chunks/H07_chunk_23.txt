Endocrine diseases increased with heat exposure, with diabetes showing significant associations with heatwaves and mortality. Previous reviews also found diabetes significantly elevated among older adults during heat exposure (Bunker et al., 2016, Song et al., 2021). Diabetes is linked to various metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurological dysfunctions, which can also impact thermoregulatory mechanisms during heat exposure (Kenny et al., 2010). In both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, the blood vessels in the skin may have reduced ability to dilate, potentially leading to decreased blood flow to the skin's surface and impairing heat dissipation (Petrofsky et al., 2005, Stansberry et al., 1997, Wick et al., 2006). Heat exposure is significantly related with kidney and urinary diseases mortality and morbidity. During further subgroup analysis, renal failure morbidity was observed to be an increased risk among older people. The kidneys play a key role in thermoregulation by regulating blood pressure and maintaining fluid and electrolyte balances (Robson, 2014). Dehydration and reduced blood flow during overheating increase the risk of kidney damage. Ageing kidneys take longer to correct imbalances under heat stress (de Lorenzo and Lia˜no, 2017, Rivas Velasquez et al., 2014). Morbidity due to mental and behavioural disorders was significantly elevated during heatwaves, while no significant associations were observed for mortality from heatwaves or morbidity during periods of high temperature. Importantly, a notable increase in mortality from intentional self-harm during high temperatures was identified, further highlighting the mental health burden of extreme temperatures. These findings align with previous meta-analytic evidence reporting increased risks of heat-related morbidity and mortality due to mental health conditions among older adults (Liu et al., 2021b). Age-related factors, along with psychotropic medications, impair the body’s ability to manage heat and increase the risk of confusion, delirium, and cognitive decline (Yezli, 2023, Maes et al., 2023). Heat exposure can also intensify psychological distress and trigger risk behaviours, including alcohol use, substance abuse, and suicidal tendencies, especially when individuals are unable to find relief from the heat (Liu et al., 2021b, Page et al., 2007). While the pooled estimate for dementia and Alzheimer’s disease during high temperatures was not statistically significant, the number of available estimates was limited. This may change as more studies emerge, given the high prevalence of these neurodegenerative conditions in older adults and their known vulnerability to heat. People aged ≥ 85 years had the highest risk overall, but for heatwaves and morbidity, the 75–84 age group showed a dramatically higher risk (116.8%, 95% CI: 24.2–278.4) compared to those aged 60–64 (34.2%, 95% CI: 21.2–48.6) and ≥ 85 years (28.9%, 95% CI: 12.7–47.4). Nevertheless, this may change with future studies due to the limited sample size for this group (n = 7). There was little difference between males and females for high-temperature morbidity and mortality. However, for heatwaves and mortality, females showed a noticeably larger increase in mortality compared to males (11.7% (95% CI: 7–16.5) vs. 6.6% (95% CI: 2.8–10.5)) respectively. This may reflect both physiological and social differences, including reduced thermoregulatory capacity due to postmenopausal changes in estrogen levels and higher prevalence of living alone, lower income, and chronic health conditions among older women, all of which have been associated with increased heat vulnerability (van Steen et al., 2019). Heatwave-related morbidity could not be analysed due to insufficient data. Climate change affects populations differently based on location. Most of the included studies were conducted in highand upper-middleincome countries, with only two from a low-income country and a few from lower-middle-income countries. This imbalance may limit the generalisability of the findings to lower-income settings, which are more vulnerable to extreme heat due to limited resources and inadequate infrastructure, such as air conditioning, home insulation, and healthcare access (Bowen and Friel, 2012, Gamble et al., 2013). The majority of studies in this meta-analysis were conducted in Climate Zone C (Mediterranean, Oceanic, Subtropical). Tropical regions had the highest morbidity risk from elevated temperatures, while Mediterranean climates recorded the highest mortality rates. Heatwaverelated morbidity was most pronounced in subtropical climates, and heatwave-related mortality was highest in tropical climates. These findings indicate that mortality and morbidity risks vary across K¨oppenGeiger climate classifications, potentially related to local climatic conditions or factors increasing vulnerability, such as socioeconomic status and healthcare infrastructure. Furthermore, humidity is another key factor, as it exacerbates heat stress and may contribute to regional differences in heat-related health outcomes (Patel et al., 2019a, Longden, 2019). Sensitivity analyses showed virtually identical high-temperature effects on morbidity (no humidity adjustment: RR 1.019 [95% CI: 1.008–1.030] vs. adjusted: RR 1.015 [95% CI: 1.009–1.020]) and mortality (no adjustment: 1.028 [95% CI: 1.022–1.034] vs. adjusted: 1.021 [95% CI: 1.018–1.024]). For heatwaves, morbidity risks were modestly higher in studies without humidity adjustment (RR 1.247 [95% CI: 1.097–1.418] vs. adjusted: 1.107 [95% CI: 1.072–1.144]), while mortality estimates remained similar (RR 1.053 [95% CI: 1.025–1.082] vs. adjusted: 1.134 [95% CI: 1.112–1.157]). These comparisons indicate that humidity adjustment or inclusion does not have a large impact on our overall temperature–health associations for morbidity and mortality (Appendix 1; Tables S11-S14). Nevertheless, humidity plays a critical role in amplifying physiological heat strain. Epidemiological studies may underestimate its effect due to methodological limitations, limited geographical coverage and data constraints, particularly the use of daily data and populations with impaired sweating responses. Moreover, at high temperatures, humidity may have limited additional influence on the health conditions that cause most heat-related deaths (Baldwin et al., 2023). Thus, the apparent similarity in effect estimates in our synthesis may reflect these analytical constraints rather than the absence of a physiological effect. In addition, we explored heatwave intensity and duration gradients using subgroup meta-analyses. These analyses showed generally higher pooled risks for more intense and longer heatwave events for mortality, but effect estimates did not consistently increase across all intensity or duration categories, particularly for morbidity outcomes (Appendix 1, Tables S13 and S14). Accordingly, the presence of an exposure–response gradient was interpreted as supportive of an association, but not as evidence of a strictly monotonic increase in risk. Such patterns may be influenced by mortality and morbidity displacement, population adaptation, and differences in target effect estimands across studies. Age-related impairments in thermoregulation, such as reduced sweat production and diminished blood flow to the skin, decrease the body's ability to dissipate heat, increasing the likelihood of cardiovascular complications. Furthermore, the combination of multiple chronic conditions and medications exacerbates the risk of adverse health outcomes in response to heat (Bunker et al., 2016, Meade et al., 2020). Other crucial factors that determine vulnerability are living alone, housing with inadequate cooling or heating, behavioural responses to heat stress, and poor access to health care (Bunker et al., 2016). 4.4. Uncertainty and heterogeneity in studies Inter-study heterogeneity was generally high when assessing the relationship between morbidity and heat exposures, indicating inherent differences between the studies. Some subgroup analyses showed low to moderate heterogeneity, while others   such as disease groups, healthcare utilisation, study designs, and heatwave intensities   had higher heterogeneity. Despite adjustments for meteorological factors and air pollutants in most studies, variations in covariates, study populations, research methods, and contexts likely contributed to the heterogeneity. For mortality and heat exposures, heterogeneity was low to moderate. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses indicated consistency in the direction and magnitude of the effects, especially for high temperatures and mortality, which showed consistently low or no observed heterogeneity, indicating high consistency among studies. Factors like age structure, access to medical care, social services, housing quality, and air conditioning likely further amplified these differences in heterogeneity (Bunker et al., 2016). Additionally, morbidity studies can have greater variability due to a wider range of health outcomes, underreporting, and limited access to care, unlike mortality studies which often have more uniform endpoints. Observations that vulnerable people often die instead of being hospitalised during heat events may explain why heat impacts on morbidity appear smaller and more variable than on mortality (Bunker et al., 2016, Lee et al., 2024, Michelozzi et al., 2009, Turner et al., 2012). Differences in study aims, effect definitions, and analytic choices imply that studies may target different underlying effects, making heterogeneity in effect estimates expected rather than indicative of low strength of evidence. Study-specific covariate structures could not be reweighted or standardised across all included studies, which may contribute to residual confounding and heterogeneity. Finally, the absence of upgrading for “large magnitude of effect” reflects the conservative Navigation Guide/GRADE criterion, which reserves this domain for unusually strong relative effects rather than for modest but widespread risks. In the context of heat and heatwaves, relative risks well below two can still translate into large population‑attributable burdens because a high proportion of the population is exposed. Global assessments have documented substantial and increasing burdens of heat‑related mortality and morbidity associated with recent human‑induced warming, illustrating how modest relative risks at the study level can generate large absolute impacts when exposure is ubiquitous (Vicedo-Cabrera et al., 2021, Zhou et al., 2024, Ebi et al., 2021, Romanello et al., 2021). 4.5.
