This review also stratified outcomes by sex and within older age groups (e.g., 60–74, 75–84, and ≥ 85 years), to better understand who is most at risk. Morbidity increased by 1.6% (95% CI:1.2–1.9) per 1◦C rise in temperature and by 18.9% (95% CI:14.6–23.3) during heatwaves, with notable elevations in heat-related illnesses and external causes for both exposures. Mortality rose by 2.2% (95% CI: 2–2.4) per 1◦C increase and by 11.7% (95% CI:10.5–12.9) during heatwaves. The leading causes of excess risk differed by exposure but were highest for external causes including heat-related illnesses and intentional self-harm, as well as cerebrovascular and endocrine diseases. Only four studies quantified the economic burden of heat on healthcare systems, showing significant cost increases for emergency department visits, respiratory admissions and ureter calculus hospitalisations (Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2021). 4.2. Context with previous evidence The health impacts of extreme heat on older adults are widely recognised, yet age-specific quantitative syntheses remain scarce. Only few systematic reviews have specifically concentrated on older adults, providing quantitative analyses of heat-related disease and mortality outcomes. Bunker et al. undertaken in 2016, is the only systematic review to date that meta-analysed 37 studies to quantify cause-specific heat effects on both mortality and morbidity across multiple disease categories in older populations. They found that per 1◦C increase in temperature, mortality increased by 3.44% (95% CI: 3.10–3.78) for cardiovascular, 3.60% (95% CI: 3.18–4.02) for respiratory, and 1.40% (95% CI: 0.06–2.75) for cerebrovascular diseases. For morbidity, the largest increases were observed for heat-related illnesses (14.83%, 95% CI: 8.22–21.84), respiratory diseases (2.76%, 95% CI: 1.51–4.03), diabetes (1.02%, 95% CI: 0.43–1.62), and acute renal failure (2.12%, 95% CI: 1.59–2.65). However, the effects of heatwaves were not investigated, nor were risk of bias evaluations or climatic and geographic variability addressed. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Byun et al. (2024) synthesised evidence on high temperatures and heatwaves in relation to mental and neurological outcomes, including studies with older populations. Their meta-analysis of five studies found that each 1◦C increase in temperature was associated with an increase in hospital visits for mental disorders (RR: 1.014, 95% CI: 1.001–1.026). For heatwaves, the pooled effect estimates showed a rise in hospital visits (RR: 1.269, 95% CI: 1.030–1.564), while mortality from mental disorders showed a similar pattern (RR: 1.266, 95% CI: 0.956–1.678), although this was not statistically significant. The review also identified substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, especially regarding heatwave definitions and temperature thresholds. Furthermore, a 2012 meta-analysis estimated a 2–5% rise in all-cause mortality per 1◦C among those ≥ 65 years (Yu et al., 2012) and narrative reviews have reported consistent heat-wave–related increases in cardiovascular and respiratory mortality (Åstr¨om et al., 2011, Kaltsatou A, 2018). Our updated findings are broadly consistent with previous systematic reviews and show similar magnitudes for key outcomes in older populations, particularly for cardiovascular, respiratory, and cerebrovascular conditions. Notably, we also identified additional causespecific risks, including e.g. external causes, intentional self-harm, digestive diseases, COPD, asthma, and dementia/Alzheimer’s disease, that were not previously synthesised. These likely reflect both the expansion in the available evidence and a growing recognition of nontraditional heat-related health outcomes. Overall, our pooled estimate of a 2.2% (95% CI: 2–2.4) increase in mortality per 1◦C rise in temperature falls within the 2–5% range reported by Yu et al (2012). These differences in outcomes may be explained by the substantially larger number of included studies, many using recent multi-city datasets with adaptive threshold modelling and the inclusion of a broader, more up-to-date evidence base. This reflects the expanded scope of available research and captures a wider spectrum of heat-related morbidity and mortality, including more recent and previously underrepresented health outcomes. Despite growing evidence on the health risks of heat, studies quantifying the economic burden remain sparse. A 2019 systematic review identified only 10 studies on heat-related healthcare costs, with just one focusing specifically on older adults (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Reported costs included ambulance services, emergency department visits, hospital admissions, medical devices, and diagnostic procedures (Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2021). These data suggest that the economic impact of extreme heat on healthcare systems is substantial yet under-investigated in older populations. Our review identified only three studies quantifying healthcare costs in the context of heat, all reporting significant increases in expenses related to respiratory admissions and emergency visits. However, there is still no comprehensive synthesis that quantifies heat-attributable costs by disease type, age group, or climate zone. This underscores the need for more targeted economic evaluations, particularly as the ageing population continues to grow and climate-related health risks intensify. 4.3. Subgroup analysis and potential underlying mechanism External causes of morbidity, particularly heat-related illnesses such as heatstroke, heat exhaustion, and dehydration, significantly increased during heatwaves, confirming previous findings (Faurie et al., 2022, Bunker et al., 2016). Prolonged exposure to high temperatures can overwhelm the body’s ability to cool itself, leading to fluid and salt loss and symptoms like nausea, confusion, and seizures. If untreated, this can result in multi-organ failure (Hifumi et al., 2018). Older adults, with diminished thirst perception and widespread use of medications like diuretics, are at greater risk of dehydration (Meade et al., 2020). CVDs, particularly cerebrovascular conditions, were strongly associated with heat exposure and mortality. This finding was in line with previous studies (Bunker et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2022, Cheng et al., 2019) and could be explained by heat exposure increasing skin blood flow and sweating, leading to volume depletion, sympathetic nervous system activation, and elevated heart rate (Crandall and Wilson, 2015). This is particularly challenging for older adults, as their physiological thermoregulatory capacity decreases with age. Specifically, sweat gland output becomes less effective, blood flow to the skin is reduced, and increases in cardiac output are smaller (Meade et al., 2020). This raises metabolic demand, potentially causing ischemia or thrombosis (Peters and Schneider, 2021). Fluid loss and electrolyte imbalances further increase the risk of arrhythmias and myocardial ischemia (Liu et al., 2015).. Older people are also more likely to take medications that can impair thermoregulatory responses such as antihypertensives, or diuretics that increase water excretion and thus predispose to dehydration. These medications are linked to increased mortality during higher temperatures (Ebi et al., 2021). The higher CVD mortality compared to morbidity may result from several factors, including the possibility that patients with severe CVD die before receiving medical care. Limited access to healthcare and cases that do not result in hospitalisation or emergency department visits can contribute to underreporting of morbidity, especially during heat periods (Lee et al., 2024, Michelozzi et al., 2009). Additionally, older adults face heightened risk due to multiple health conditions, increased S. Günsche et al. To better capture the effects of temperature on morbidity, data from outpatient settings, such as general practices and home visits, should be included, as they are currently underrepresented (Bunker et al., 2016, Smith et al., 2016). Respiratory mortality and morbidity were significantly elevated, consistent with previous studies (Achebak et al., 2024, Bunker et al., 2016). The mechanisms leading to adverse respiratory outcomes are not fully understood but are likely influenced by thermoregulatory pathways (Michelozzi et al., 2009, Achebak et al., 2024). Heat exposure can worsen conditions like COPD by increasing ventilation and inflammatory responses (Viegi et al., 2007, Anderson et al., 2013, White, 2006, Leon and Helwig, 2010) COPD is a common medical condition among older people (Bunker et al., 2016, Mollica et al., 2021). This may lead to hyperventilation during extreme heat, increasing the risk of hyperinflation, dyspnea, and cardiovascular complications (Sprung, 1980). Inhalation of hot air can also trigger adverse airway reactions (Anderson et al., 2013) and cause bronchoconstriction in patients with asthma (Hayes et al., 2012). However, this study found a significant effect for COPD morality per 1 degree increase but not for morbidity. No significant effects were found for asthma, or pneumonia, though the overall respiratory impact was notable across all exposures and outcomes. Endocrine diseases increased with heat exposure, with diabetes showing significant associations with heatwaves and mortality. Previous reviews also found diabetes significantly elevated among older adults during heat exposure (Bunker et al., 2016, Song et al., 2021). Diabetes is linked to various metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurological dysfunctions, which can also impact thermoregulatory mechanisms during heat exposure (Kenny et al., 2010). In both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, the blood vessels in the skin may have reduced ability to dilate, potentially leading to decreased blood flow to the skin's surface and impairing heat dissipation (Petrofsky et al., 2005, Stansberry et al., 1997, Wick et al., 2006). Heat exposure is significantly related with kidney and urinary diseases mortality and morbidity. During further subgroup analysis, renal failure morbidity was observed to be an increased risk among older people. The kidneys play a key role in thermoregulation by regulating blood pressure and maintaining fluid and electrolyte balances (Robson, 2014). Dehydration and reduced blood flow during overheating increase the risk of kidney damage. Ageing kidneys take longer to correct imbalances under heat stress (de Lorenzo and Lia˜no, 2017, Rivas Velasquez et al., 2014). Morbidity due to mental and behavioural disorders was significantly elevated during heatwaves, while no significant associations were observed for mortality from heatwaves or morbidity during periods of high temperature.
