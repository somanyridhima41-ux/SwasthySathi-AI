3. Results 3.1. Study selection and characteristics The database searches retrieved 21,610 records. After filtering nonEnglish, non-human or non-journal articles and removing duplicates 12,131 records remained for title and abstract screening. Most records were excluded at this stage as they were unrelated to heat exposure and health outcomes, were the wrong publication type or not based on human data. Hence, 812 studies were assessed in full text. A further 210 studies were excluded, primarily due to irrelevant outcomes or exposures, unsuitable study design, or populations outside the inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1 for details). An additional 21 studies were identified through the screening of 25 relevant systematic reviews, which had been retrieved via database searches and included data on older adults, suggesting the primary search was comprehensive. In total, 623 studies met the inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1). Table 2 provides an overview of the 623 included studies, categorised by exposure type and reported outcome. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted for morbidity and mortality outcomes in relation to high temperature and heatwave exposure (n = 578). An additional 43 studies, which reported high temperature effects using non-standardizable exposure metrics (e.g. high temperature vs. study-defined reference such as minimum risk or percentile-based values), were synthesised in a supplementary metaanalysis (Appendix 1, Tables S16 and S17). A meta-analysis for healthcare costs was not feasible due to the limited number of eligible studies. Four studies reported healthcare costs; two of them also contributed to the meta-analysis as they reported morbidity and heat exposure outcomes alongside cost estimates, while two were included in the narrative synthesis only. Key findings from all included studies are summarised in the supplementary material (Appendix 2). The included studies were published between 1995 and 2025 and were conducted across Asia, Africa, Australia, Europe, North America, and South America. There were 154 location-specific studies for mortality and 151 for morbidity, which could be classified by the K¨oppenGeiger climate zones: A (tropical), B (arid), C (temperate–including Mediterranean, Oceanic, and subtropical climates), D (continental), and E (polar) (Peel et al., 2007). Most studies were conducted in temperate (C) and continental (D) climates, primarily across North America, Europe, and East Asia (Fig. 2). A complete list of all study countries and locations is provided in Appendix 1, Table S8. 3.2. Overall, most studies showed low or probably low RoB in outcome assessment, selection bias, missing data, selective reporting, conflict of interest, and other sources of bias. However, higher RoB was frequently observed for exposure assessment and confounding bias, often due to the use of single monitoring stations for a large geographical area, lack of population weighting, or inadequate adjustment for critical time-varying confounders such as seasonality or humidity. This pattern held across most exposure–outcome categories, with the majority of studies rated as low or probably low overall RoB, and only a small proportion judged as probably high or high. Summary plots were created using the Risk of Bias Visualization Tool (ROBVIS) to illustrate the percentage of RoB ratings across domains for each exposure–outcome combination (Fig. 3) (McGuinness and Higgins, 2020). Synthesis of high temperature effects Overall, a 1◦C rise in temperature increased morbidity (RR 1.016 [CI95% 1.012–1.019]) (Fig. 4) and mortality (RR 1.022 [CI95% 1.020–1.024]) among older individuals (Fig. 5). The highest morbidity risks were linked to heat-related illnesses (RR 1.228 [CI95% 1.155–1.307]), external causes (RR 1.109 [CI95% 1.071–1.149]), and endocrine diseases (RR 1.035 [CI95% 1.010–1.059]), while cardiovascular, digestive, infectious, and mental diseases showed no significant associations. Individuals aged ≥ 85 years, those in tropical climates, lower-middle-income countries, and residents of Asia experienced the largest increases in morbidity per 1◦C rise (Fig. 4). Mortality risk was mostly elevated for intentional self-harm (RR 1.068 [CI95% 1.026–1.113]), external causes (RR 1.039 [CI95% 1.016–1.063]), COPD (RR 1.038 [CI95% 1.001–1.076]), cerebrovascular (RR 1.031 [CI95% 1.012–1.049]), respiratory (RR 1.029 [CI95% 1.019–1.039]) and cardiovascular diseases (RR 1.021 [CI95% 1.014–1.027]) (Fig. 5). The overall test of heterogeneity was statistically significant for continent (QM (df = 5) = 24.8609, p = 0.0001, I2 = 0.0%), age group (QM (df = 2) = 11.7780, p = 0.0028, I2 = 0.0%), and hemisphere (QM (df = 1) = 11.3437, p = 0.0008), suggesting that these factors may significantly modify the association between high temperatures and mortality. Among continents, Australia showed the strongest increase in mortality (RR 1.075 [95% CI 1.052–1.098], p < 0.0001), followed by Europe (RR 1.019 [95% CI 1.012–1.027], p < 0.0001) Asia (RR 1.010 [95% CI 1.002–1.018], p = 0.0129), and North America (RR 1.009 [95% CI 1.002–1.015], p = 0.0069). Age-stratified effects showed significantly increased mortality for the reference group aged 60–74 years (RR 1.015 [95% CI 1.009–1.022], p < 0.0001) and those aged ≥ 85 years (RR 1.018 [95% CI 1.008–1.029], p = 0.0006). Hemispheres showed significantly elevated mortality risks. The Northern Hemisphere (reference) had an effect estimate of RR 1.021 [95% CI 1.018–1.024], p < 0.0001, while the Southern Hemisphere showed a comparable effect (RR 1.021 [95% CI 1.009–1.033], p = 0.0008) (Appendix 1, Table S9). Synthesis of heatwave effects Heatwaves significantly amplified morbidity (RR 1.189 [CI95% 1.146–1.233]), with the most substantial increases during moderateintensity heatwaves (RR 1.229 [CI95% 1.078–1.401]) and heatwaves lasting for ≥ 2 days (RR 1.212 [CI95% 1.137–1.292]) (Fig. 6). The largest increases were observed for external causes (RR 2.371 [CI95% 1.764–3.187]), and heat-related illnesses (RR 3.693 [CI95% 2.361–5.778]). High risks were also noted for endocrine diseases (RR 1.176 [CI95% 1.077–1.285]), fluid and electrolyte disorders (RR 1.155 [CI95% 1.057–1.262]) and renal failure (RR 1.146 [CI95% 1.041–1.262]). Subtropical climates, Australia, and high-income countries experienced the greatest morbidity risks. (See Fig. 6) Meta-regression identified age group as a significant risk factor (QM (df = 2) = 7.9391, p = 0.0189, I2 = 94.82%). The highest morbidity risk was observed in the 75–84 age group (RR 1.513 [95% CI 1.103–2.074], p = 0.0101), compared to the reference group (60–74 years). No significant difference was found for individuals aged ≥ 85 years (RR 0.953 [95% CI 0.792–1.147], p = 0.6106). However, the limited number of studies (n = 7) for the 75–84 group may affect reliability.
