In the context of population ageing and climate change, which is expected to make extreme heat events more intense, frequent, prolonged, and widespread, understanding their health impacts on older adults is crucial for informing targeted interventions. Methods: This systematic review and meta-analysis searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and AgeLine for studies published between January 1, 1990, and March 15, 2025. Original research articles assessing morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs among older adults during periods of high temperatures or heatwaves were included. Eligible studies used observational designs, such as time series, case-crossover, or case time-series, comparing risks across different exposures or time periods. Reviews, commentaries, grey literature, animal studies, and studies not focused on temperature as the primary exposure were excluded. Risk estimates were extracted from published studies. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to pool relative risks of morbidity and mortality, focusing on specific disease categories according to ICD-10 codes, climate zones, and demographic factors. The certainty and strength of the evidence were assessed following the Navigation Guide systematic review methodology framework. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023423311). Results: Out of 21,610 identified studies, 623 were eligible, spanning six continents and 305 locations classified by K¨oppen climate zones. Of these, 578 were included in the meta-analysis, 43 in a supplementary meta-analysis, and four on healthcare costs were narratively synthesised. A 1◦C temperature rise was associated with higher morbidity (RR 1.016 [CI95% 1.012–1.019]) and mortality (RR 1.022 [CI95% 1.020–1.024]) among older people. The highest morbidity risks were linked to heat-related illnesses, external causes and endocrine diseases, and in tropical climates. Mortality risk was highest for intentional self-harm and external causes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and in Mediterranean climates, with the strongest overall impact on those aged ≥ 85 years. Heatwaves increased morbidity (RR 1.189 [CI95% 1.146–1.233]), particularly for heat-related illnesses and external causes, with the highest overall risk seen in adults aged 75–84 and in subtropical climates. Mortality also rose (RR 1.117 [CI95% 1.105–1.129]), especially for cerebrovascular, endocrine and respiratory diseases, with the greatest overall risks found in tropical climates and among individuals aged ≥ 85 years. Despite the limited number of studies on healthcare costs they showed significant increases in costs during heat exposure for emergency department presentations, respiratory and ureter calculus hospitalisations. Effective, evidence-based interventions, such as tailored heat health action plans that consider individual risk factors, local resources, climatic characteristics and healthcare infrastructure, are crucial to minimise these risks. 1. Introduction The anticipated increase in chronic and acute heat exposure among older adults poses a significant global public health challenge (Falchetta et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2022, Bunker et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2021b, Lee and Kim, 2016). Extreme heat increases mortality and morbidity, particularly in older individuals (Bunker et al., 2016, Faurie et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2021b, Liu et al., 2022, Lee and Kim, 2016, Ciss´e et al., 2022). It also imposes a significant economic burden on healthcare systems, with most healthcare costs attributed to older people (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Older adults account for a large proportion of overall healthcare costs due to their higher prevalence of chronic diseases and healthcare utilisation (Kallestrup-Lamb et al., 2024, OECD 2023, WHO 2025). Age-related comorbidities, medication use, compromised physiological function, and financial constraints place many older adults at greater risk of the negative health effects of heat exposure (Zhang et al., 2016). The global population aged 65 years and older is projected to increase from 761 million in 2021 to about 1.6 billion by 2050 (United Nations, 2023). The Global Burden of Disease Study in 2019, identified cardiovascular diseases, neoplasms, and chronic respiratory diseases, with ischemic heart disease, stroke, COPD, dementia, and lower respiratory infections as top causes of death among those aged 70 and older (GBD 2019 Ageing Collaborators, 2022). These climatic and demographic changes will likely lead to increases in both acute and chronic heat-sensitive diseases, presenting significant challenges to healthcare systems (Bunker et al., 2016, United Nations, 2023, Liu et al., 2021a, Liu et al., 2021b, Liu et al., 2022). Although studies on healthcare costs related to heat exposure are limited, existing literature suggests the economic impact on the healthcare system is substantial (Wondmagegn et al., 2019, Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2021). A 2019 systematic review on heat-attributable healthcare costs encompassed ten studies, but only one specifically addressed healthcare costs among older adults (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Heat-related healthcare expenses include ambulance services, emergency department visits, hospital admissions, medical devices, and diagnostic procedures (Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Wondmagegn et al., 2019, Lin et al., 2012). Estimating these costs offers an economic viewpoint on the burden of heat-related health effects (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Previous reviews have explored disease-specific heat-associated risks, such as cardiovascular diseases (Liu et al., 2022, Cheng et al., 2019), mental health conditions (Liu et al., 2021b, Byun et al., 2024), heat-related illnesses (Faurie et al., 2022), renal diseases (Liu et al., 2021a), diabetes (Song et al., 2021) and respiratory diseases (Cheng et al., 2019, Tran et al., 2025), finding older people to be at risk for both mortality and morbidity. However, few systematic reviews focused specifically on older people, quantifying heat-related morbidity and mortality outcomes. A 2016 meta-analysis showed that for every 1◦C increase in temperature, mortality significantly increased for cardiovascular (3.44%), respiratory (3.60%), and cerebrovascular diseases (1.40%). Similarly, a 1◦C increase in temperature was associated with higher morbidity for respiratory diseases (1.65%), diabetes (1.02%), genitourinary diseases (2.12%), and heat-related illnesses (14.83%) (Bunker et al., 2016). A 2011 meta-analysis indicated that individuals aged 65 and older experienced a 2–5% increase in all-cause mortality for each 1◦C rise in temperature (Yu et al., 2012). However, neither metaanalysis assessed the impact of heatwave exposure, explored climatic characteristics, nor conducted risk of bias (RoB), certainty, or strength of evidence assessments. A more recent meta-analysis in 2024 specifically investigated mental and neurological outcomes in older adults and included both high temperature and heatwave exposures. It reported elevated risks for mental disorders during heat events and applied a RoB assessment (Byun et al., 2024). However, the analysis did not account for differences across climate zones and income classifications. While systematic reviews and meta-analyses have considered the impact of climate variability on these associations, the risk of heat-related mortality and morbidity across different climate zones remains unassessed. Other reviews synthesised previous findings narratively (Åstr¨om et al., 2011, Kaltsatou A, 2018). Moreover, many studies on the relationship between heat and health have been published since. No previous review has comprehensively synthesised mortality, morbidity, and healthcare cost outcomes in older populations, nor provided stratified analyses by climate zones or demographic subgroups. This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses these gaps by quantifying the effects of heat exposure, including high temperatures and heatwaves, on mortality, morbidity, and healthcare costs among older adults based on available epidemiological evidence. It focuses on specific disease categories according to ICD-10, climate zones, and demographic factors, while also assessing risk of bias, overall certainty, and strength of the evidence. 2. Methods This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Appendix 1, Table S1) (Page et al., 2021). The protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023423311). The primary research question guiding this systematic review and meta-analysis is framed using the PECO structure (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcomes) (Morgan et al., 2018) as follows: Among older adults (P), is exposure to high ambient temperatures or heatwaves (E), associated with increased risk of mortality, morbidity, or healthcare costs (O), compared with cooler reference periods or nonheatwave days (C)? 2.1.
