Differences in study aims, effect definitions, and analytic choices imply that studies may target different underlying effects, making heterogeneity in effect estimates expected rather than indicative of low strength of evidence. Study-specific covariate structures could not be reweighted or standardised across all included studies, which may contribute to residual confounding and heterogeneity. Finally, the absence of upgrading for “large magnitude of effect” reflects the conservative Navigation Guide/GRADE criterion, which reserves this domain for unusually strong relative effects rather than for modest but widespread risks. In the context of heat and heatwaves, relative risks well below two can still translate into large population‑attributable burdens because a high proportion of the population is exposed. Global assessments have documented substantial and increasing burdens of heat‑related mortality and morbidity associated with recent human‑induced warming, illustrating how modest relative risks at the study level can generate large absolute impacts when exposure is ubiquitous (Vicedo-Cabrera et al., 2021, Zhou et al., 2024, Ebi et al., 2021, Romanello et al., 2021). 4.5. Strengths and limitations This review included 623 studies, providing sufficient evidence to analyse a wide range of climate zones, income settings, and disease outcomes. This enabled detailed stratification by age, sex, climate classification, and income group. The limited number of additional studies identified through citation screening of existing reviews further supports the comprehensiveness of the original search strategy. This study had several limitations. Only peer-reviewed journal articles published in English were included. Significant publication bias and high heterogeneity were detected, indicating the possibility of unexamined factors. Effect estimates were standardised to reflect per 1◦C increases for comparability, but these increments are defined relative to each study’s own metric and threshold, and the ranges and interpretations of these metrics (for example, dry‑bulb temperature vs composite indices that incorporate humidity) could differ across studies. Consequently, a 1◦C increase in one metric is not physically equivalent to a 1◦C increase in another and our pooled ‘per 1◦C’ estimates should be interpreted as standardised statistical contrasts rather than strictly comparable physical exposure changes. In addition, standardisation was based on high‑temperature thresholds defined in the original studies, not across the full exposure range, so the pooled estimates do not imply a fully linear exposure–response relationship. For heat-wave studies, recent methodological work indicates that non–heat-wave temperatures can act as confounders and that the choice of temperature-adjustment strategy can substantially influence estimated heat-wave effects (Kim and Bell, 2024). In our review, most studies estimating heat-wave–health associations did not explicitly adjust for non–heat-wave-related temperatures; therefore, the pooled heat-wave estimates may plausibly underestimate the total health effects attributable to heat waves. This highlights the importance of careful analytical design and transparent reporting of temperatureadjustment strategies in future heat-wave epidemiological studies. Estimates were also pooled across different disease groups to generate overall effects; however, cause-specific subgroup analyses were conducted to address variation in disease pathways and reporting. While many subgroups included a large number of estimates, others had fewer and should be interpreted with caution. To avoid bias and over-representation of correlated estimates (e.g. multiple age groups or lag days within the same study), we summarised these using a fixed-effect model to obtain a single study-level estimate. This approach assumes that subgroup estimates reflect a common underlying effect within each study and provides an appropriate weighting of evidence without over-representing individual studies. While this may slightly smooth or attenuate subgroup-specific differences, it yields a more stable and interpretable pooled estimate (Riley et al., 2015, Borenstein, 2009, Deeks et al., 2024). Given the large number of included studies, it was not feasible to assess every study-specific detail in the risk-of-bias evaluation. A consistent approach was applied across all studies, focusing on key domains such as exposure, outcome, and confounding. This ensured comparability but may have overlooked finer differences in exposure precision, outcome definitions, or covariate adjustment. However, the overall assessment captures the best available reflection of study quality based on reported information. Despite including studies from various locations, there were limited numbers from lowand middle-income countries, tropical and polar regions, the African continent and South America. Studies from Africa were largely absent, while those from South America were limited to a few locations. These regions may experience greater vulnerability to the impacts of a warming climate among older populations, although vulnerability factors themselves were not directly assessed in this review. Additionally, most of the original studies lacked data on modifying factors such as medication use, pre-existing conditions, and comorbidities. Finally, this review included only four studies investigating heat-driven healthcare costs, limiting the strength of the evidence on this association. 5. Conclusion Our findings underscore the urgent need for climate policies and tailored health interventions to mitigate the escalating morbidity and mortality risks among older adults. With global climate change expected to increase acute heat exposure and the growing vulnerability of an S. Günsche et al. Environment International 208 (2026) 110129 15 ageing population, certain regions will face heightened demands on social and healthcare services, necessitating innovative policy solutions (Falchetta et al., 2024). The review identified consistent associations between high temperatures, heatwaves and multiple heat-sensitive outcomes, with variation by exposure definition, outcome type, age group, sex, climate zone and national income classification. To better understand the observed variations across exposures, populations and regions, studies should also explore the underlying factors and local resources that influence vulnerability and adaptive capacity. This could include individual-level data on pre-existing conditions, medication use, heat-sensitive diseases and healthcare access. Additional studies are especially needed in underrepresented regions, including the African continent and South America, as well as polar and tropical climates, and lowand middleincome countries, to understand how health risks vary across populations. Finally, few studies have quantified the healthcare-related economic burden of heat, despite its clear challenges on healthcare systems. Further cost-focused research is critical to inform policy and support the implementation of sustainable and equitable heat mitigation strategies.
