Additionally, 80% prediction intervals (PI) were used to estimate the range within which the true RR for future studies is likely to fall, with severe heterogeneity indicated if the 80% PI included the null effect and was more than twice the width of the 95% CIs (IntHout et al., 2016). Publication bias was evaluated using Egger's test, funnel plots (Egger et al., 1997), and the trim-and-fill method (Duval and Tweedie, 2004). A univariate meta-regression analysis was performed to explore studylevel characteristics that may explain between-study heterogeneity in effect sizes (Liu et al., 2022, Bown and Sutton, 2010, Deeks et al., 2024). The variables tested included K¨oppen-Geiger climate group, lag effects, age group, sex, continent, hemisphere, heatwave intensity and duration, and income classification. Sensitivity analyses were undertaken by categorising studies according to temperature metrics, study design, lag periods, overall RoB rating, seasonal variations (warm season vs. whole year), air pollution and humidity adjustment, as well as modelling approach (non-linear or linear) (Liu et al., 2022). Leave-one-out analyses were used to assess the impact of individual studies on pooled RRs. Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.1) (R Core Team, 2024) with the package metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010). 2.6. Certainty of the evidence The certainty of the evidence was assessed following the Navigation Guide for systematic reviews of observational studies in environmental health (Johnson et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2016). This process involved three steps, with the first step—Risk of Bias (RoB) assessment—described in Section 2.4. 2) The certainty of evidence across studies was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework (Guyatt et al., 2011). Evidence for each exposure–outcome pair was initially rated as “moderate” and could be downgraded for domains such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. It could be upgraded based on evidence for a dose–response relationship, large magnitude of effect, or residual confounding that would likely attenuate the observed S. Günsche et al. Environment International 208 (2026) 110129 4 association. The final certainty rating was assigned as “high”, “moderate”, or “low”, based on the net impact of downgrading and upgrading (Johnson et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2016). The decision rules and justifications for these adjustments are provided in Appendix 1, Table S7, which are also described in detail elsewhere (Johnson et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2016). 3) The overall strength of the evidence was determined by combining certainty assessments with consistency of findings and confidence in estimated effects. Evidence was categorized as “sufficient,” “limited,” or “inadequate,” following the Navigation Guide framework (Johnson et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2016). 3. Results 3.1. Study selection and characteristics The database searches retrieved 21,610 records. After filtering nonEnglish, non-human or non-journal articles and removing duplicates 12,131 records remained for title and abstract screening. Most records were excluded at this stage as they were unrelated to heat exposure and health outcomes, were the wrong publication type or not based on human data. Hence, 812 studies were assessed in full text. A further 210 studies were excluded, primarily due to irrelevant outcomes or exposures, unsuitable study design, or populations outside the inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1 for details). An additional 21 studies were identified through the screening of 25 relevant systematic reviews, which had been retrieved via database searches and included data on older adults, suggesting the primary search was comprehensive. In total, 623 studies met the inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1). Table 2 provides an overview of the 623 included studies, categorised by exposure type and reported outcome. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted for morbidity and mortality outcomes in relation to high temperature and heatwave exposure (n = 578). An additional 43 studies, which reported high temperature effects using non-standardizable exposure metrics (e.g. high temperature vs. study-defined reference such as minimum risk or percentile-based values), were synthesised in a supplementary metaanalysis (Appendix 1, Tables S16 and S17). A meta-analysis for healthcare costs was not feasible due to the limited number of eligible studies. Four studies reported healthcare costs; two of them also contributed to the meta-analysis as they reported morbidity and heat exposure outcomes alongside cost estimates, while two were included in the narrative synthesis only. Key findings from all included studies are summarised in the supplementary material (Appendix 2). The included studies were published between 1995 and 2025 and were conducted across Asia, Africa, Australia, Europe, North America, and South America. There were 154 location-specific studies for mortality and 151 for morbidity, which could be classified by the K¨oppenGeiger climate zones: A (tropical), B (arid), C (temperate–including Mediterranean, Oceanic, and subtropical climates), D (continental), and E (polar) (Peel et al., 2007). Most studies were conducted in temperate (C) and continental (D) climates, primarily across North America, Europe, and East Asia (Fig. 2). A complete list of all study countries and locations is provided in Appendix 1, Table S8. 3.2. Synthesis of high temperature effects Overall, a 1◦C rise in temperature increased morbidity (RR 1.016 [CI95% 1.012–1.019]) (Fig. 4) and mortality (RR 1.022 [CI95% 1.020–1.024]) among older individuals (Fig. 5). The highest morbidity risks were linked to heat-related illnesses (RR 1.228 [CI95% 1.155–1.307]), external causes (RR 1.109 [CI95% 1.071–1.149]), and endocrine diseases (RR 1.035 [CI95% 1.010–1.059]), while cardiovascular, digestive, infectious, and mental diseases showed no significant associations. Individuals aged ≥ 85 years, those in tropical climates, lower-middle-income countries, and residents of Asia experienced the largest increases in morbidity per 1◦C rise (Fig. 4). Mortality risk was mostly elevated for intentional self-harm (RR 1.068 [CI95% 1.026–1.113]), external causes (RR 1.039 [CI95% 1.016–1.063]), COPD (RR 1.038 [CI95% 1.001–1.076]), cerebrovascular (RR 1.031 [CI95% 1.012–1.049]), respiratory (RR 1.029 [CI95% 1.019–1.039]) and cardiovascular diseases (RR 1.021 [CI95% 1.014–1.027]) (Fig. 5). The overall test of heterogeneity was statistically significant for continent (QM (df = 5) = 24.8609, p = 0.0001, I2 = 0.0%), age group (QM (df = 2) = 11.7780, p = 0.0028, I2 = 0.0%), and hemisphere (QM (df = 1) = 11.3437, p = 0.0008), suggesting that these factors may significantly modify the association between high temperatures and mortality.
