Overall, most studies showed low or probably low RoB in outcome assessment, selection bias, missing data, selective reporting, conflict of interest, and other sources of bias. However, higher RoB was frequently observed for exposure assessment and confounding bias, often due to the use of single monitoring stations for a large geographical area, lack of population weighting, or inadequate adjustment for critical time-varying confounders such as seasonality or humidity. This pattern held across most exposure–outcome categories, with the majority of studies rated as low or probably low overall RoB, and only a small proportion judged as probably high or high. Summary plots were created using the Risk of Bias Visualization Tool (ROBVIS) to illustrate the percentage of RoB ratings across domains for each exposure–outcome combination (Fig. 3) (McGuinness and Higgins, 2020). Synthesis of high temperature effects Overall, a 1◦C rise in temperature increased morbidity (RR 1.016 [CI95% 1.012–1.019]) (Fig. 4) and mortality (RR 1.022 [CI95% 1.020–1.024]) among older individuals (Fig. 5). The highest morbidity risks were linked to heat-related illnesses (RR 1.228 [CI95% 1.155–1.307]), external causes (RR 1.109 [CI95% 1.071–1.149]), and endocrine diseases (RR 1.035 [CI95% 1.010–1.059]), while cardiovascular, digestive, infectious, and mental diseases showed no significant associations. Individuals aged ≥ 85 years, those in tropical climates, lower-middle-income countries, and residents of Asia experienced the largest increases in morbidity per 1◦C rise (Fig. 4). Mortality risk was mostly elevated for intentional self-harm (RR 1.068 [CI95% 1.026–1.113]), external causes (RR 1.039 [CI95% 1.016–1.063]), COPD (RR 1.038 [CI95% 1.001–1.076]), cerebrovascular (RR 1.031 [CI95% 1.012–1.049]), respiratory (RR 1.029 [CI95% 1.019–1.039]) and cardiovascular diseases (RR 1.021 [CI95% 1.014–1.027]) (Fig. 5). The overall test of heterogeneity was statistically significant for continent (QM (df = 5) = 24.8609, p = 0.0001, I2 = 0.0%), age group (QM (df = 2) = 11.7780, p = 0.0028, I2 = 0.0%), and hemisphere (QM (df = 1) = 11.3437, p = 0.0008), suggesting that these factors may significantly modify the association between high temperatures and mortality. Among continents, Australia showed the strongest increase in mortality (RR 1.075 [95% CI 1.052–1.098], p < 0.0001), followed by Europe (RR 1.019 [95% CI 1.012–1.027], p < 0.0001) Asia (RR 1.010 [95% CI 1.002–1.018], p = 0.0129), and North America (RR 1.009 [95% CI 1.002–1.015], p = 0.0069). Age-stratified effects showed significantly increased mortality for the reference group aged 60–74 years (RR 1.015 [95% CI 1.009–1.022], p < 0.0001) and those aged ≥ 85 years (RR 1.018 [95% CI 1.008–1.029], p = 0.0006). Hemispheres showed significantly elevated mortality risks. The Northern Hemisphere (reference) had an effect estimate of RR 1.021 [95% CI 1.018–1.024], p < 0.0001, while the Southern Hemisphere showed a comparable effect (RR 1.021 [95% CI 1.009–1.033], p = 0.0008) (Appendix 1, Table S9). Synthesis of heatwave effects Heatwaves significantly amplified morbidity (RR 1.189 [CI95% 1.146–1.233]), with the most substantial increases during moderateintensity heatwaves (RR 1.229 [CI95% 1.078–1.401]) and heatwaves lasting for ≥ 2 days (RR 1.212 [CI95% 1.137–1.292]) (Fig. 6). The largest increases were observed for external causes (RR 2.371 [CI95% 1.764–3.187]), and heat-related illnesses (RR 3.693 [CI95% 2.361–5.778]). High risks were also noted for endocrine diseases (RR 1.176 [CI95% 1.077–1.285]), fluid and electrolyte disorders (RR 1.155 [CI95% 1.057–1.262]) and renal failure (RR 1.146 [CI95% 1.041–1.262]). Subtropical climates, Australia, and high-income countries experienced the greatest morbidity risks. (See Fig. 6) Meta-regression identified age group as a significant risk factor (QM (df = 2) = 7.9391, p = 0.0189, I2 = 94.82%). No significant difference was found for individuals aged ≥ 85 years (RR 0.953 [95% CI 0.792–1.147], p = 0.6106). However, the limited number of studies (n = 7) for the 75–84 group may affect reliability.
