These studies represented 17 countries/territories in eight sub-regions. Most (26/37) studies were conducted in Asia. More than half (20/37) of the studies only covered flood events within one year (Fig. 2B). Almost all studies were restricted to the impacts within one state/ province (Table 1), except for five estimating the impacts of flood events across Ireland (Boudou et al., 2021), mainland China (Guo et al., 2020), Thailand (Suwanpakdee et al., 2015), Vietnam (Phung et al., 2021), and the United Kingdom (Milojevic et al., 2011), respectively. Time-series study was the most widely used study design (Fig. 2C). 3.1.1. Outcome ascertainments A variety of morbidities were assessed as well as all-cause, cardiovascular (CVD), and respiratory mortality. Except for two studies ascertaining outcomes with standardized tools (Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 2011, 2016), and three through self-report (Mulder et al., 2019; Milojevic et al., 2012; Pradhan et al., 2007), almost all (32/37) studies were based on administrative datasets (Table 1). Among the studies employing an administrative dataset, some (9/32) only covered a part of the general population because the datasets were from a simple source (e.g., datasets of hospital visits, emergency visits, or admissions); however, most (23/32) were based on a comprehensive source (e.g., diseases surveillance network, whole-population insurance database, or mortality registration) and so comprehensively captured the incidences of outcomes in the general population. 3.1.2. Exposure assessments The exposure assessment method varied importantly across studies (Table 1). Almost all studies defined the exposed population as those residing in flooded areas during flooded periods. On the temporal scale, exposure to floods was defined as days, weeks, months, seasons, years, or pregnancy periods, with the flood events based on disaster records (Table 1). Using disaster records or interviews, exposure to floods was defined on a spatial scale as individuals residing in households (Milojevic et al., 2012; Miyamori et al., 2023; Yoshida et al., 2021; Vollaard et al., 2004), communities/village/district/town (Boudou et al., 2021; Suwanpakdee et al., 2015; Milojevic et al., 2011; Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 2011, 2016; Pradhan et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2021a; Elsanousi et al., 2018; Vanasse et al., 2016; Deng et al., 2022), cities (Yan et al., 2020; Hetherington et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2016, 2019, 2020; Zhang et al., 2016a, 2016b, 2019; Hu et al., 2018b; Huang et al., 2016; Wade et al., 2014; Ni et al., 2014; Lan et al., 2022), or provinces with flood events (Guo et al., 2020; Phung et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2011). The data used for these assessments were from sources including official records, insurance certifications, and surveys/interviews. Almost all studies in mainland China determined flooded areas and periods by referring to the records provided by Yearbooks of Meteorological Disasters, where flood periods were defined as days with cumulative rainfall of >80 mm for three consecutive days (Ma et al., 2021). Studies in Japan defined flooded households as those destroyed or whose primary family caregiver was seriously injured or lost jobs due to flood events, which were certified by a government-run insurance system (Miyamori et al., 2023; Yoshida et al., 2021). A study in Canada ascertained flooded areas using water level data from governmental hydraulic records (Vanasse et al., 2016). Flooded households were identified through interviews in three studies (Mulder et al., 2019; Milojevic et al., 2012; Vollaard et al., 2004). Some studies used a distinctive definition of flood exposure. A study in Uganda defined flooded villages as those containing or were along flooding rivers (Boyce et al., 2016); a study in the USA defined exposure group as the population residing in postcode areas where at least one-third of the area was flooded (Quist et al., 2022); a study in Cambodia quantified exposure as square kilometers of floods in the district of residence (Saulnier et al., 2018). 3.1.3. Risk of bias We judged exposure assessment and confounding as the main risks of bias (Table S2), with 13 and 18 included studies judged as at a probably or definitely high RoB for these domains respectively. Potential bias in the exposure assessments arose from the time frames used (weeks/ months/seasons/years), which could have resulted in misclassifying the periods before flood events as exposed periods. Potential bias from confounding arose from inadequate control of confounders in the analysis. 3.2. Associations with mortality Three studies investigated the risk of all-cause mortality (i.e., deaths from all causes) related to floods in the <6 month time interval (Fig. 3). (Yan et al., 2020; Milojevic et al., 2012; Pradhan et al., 2007) The meta-analysis of the three studies yielded an overall RR of 1.26 (1.10, 1.46), where the time frames of the estimates varied from four days to 24 weeks, with some inconsistency (I2 = 30%, p-value of test for heterogeneity = 0.24). Only one study in the UK examined the effect of flood in the ≥6 month time interval, finding that the risk of all-cause mortality decreased in one year (RR 0.90 [0.82, 0.99]) (Milojevic et al., 2011). For specific mortality outcomes (Fig. 4), the risk of CVD mortality was found to increase during 0–4 days post floods (Yan et al., 2020). However, no significant association was found for respiratory mortality (Yan et al., 2020). 3.3. Associations with morbidity The non-external hospitalization risk was found increased (RR 1.07 [1.03, 1.10]) during the four weeks post floods in Vietnam (Phung et al., 2021). Another study focused on the impacts of a flood event in Japan (2018) on population aged >45 years. The authors observed that the risk of nursing home admission was three times higher among those affected by floods compared with those not affected by floods (Fig. 4). (Miyamori et al., 2023) This finding suggested that older people might be more vulnerable to the health impacts of floods. 3.3.1. Three studies estimated the risk of overall gastrointestinal diseases in the <6 month time interval (specifically within three weeks) following two flood events in 2016 and 2018 in the USA (Quist et al., 2022), 129 events during 2002–2008 in the USA (Wade et al., 2014), and 240 events during 1998–2008 in Taiwan (Huang et al., 2016), respectively. The meta-analysis yielded an overall RR of 1.10 (1.08, 1.13), with consistent RRs (I2 = 0%, p-value of test for heterogeneity = 0.55). Four studies estimated the impact on diarrhea diseases incidence in the <6 month time interval (Milojevic et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2022). The meta-analysis of the effect estimates yielded an overall RR of 1.11 (1.04, 1.20), with no evidence of between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p-value of test for heterogeneity = 0.47). Among the studies not included in the meta-analysis, the study in Cambodia reported that the risk of diarrhea increased in the current month and third month post flood periods (Saulnier et al., 2018), and the study in China showed that the risk increased on 11th days post the flood event in 2007 (Gao et al., 2016). Only a study in China estimated the effect of flood in the ≥6 month time interval and found that the risk of diarrhea increased after a flood event in 2016 (Zhang et al., 2019). Seven studies estimated the effects on dysentery in the <6 month time interval (Liu et al., 2016, 2017, 2019; Zhang et al., 2016b; Ni et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2016). Nine effect estimates were extracted because two study provided effect estimates for two locations and two flood events, respectively (Liu et al., 2016, 2017). The meta-analysis yielded an overall RR of 1.38 (1.18, 1.62). Large between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 87%, p-value <0.01); however, all the RRs indicated flood events were associated with an increased risk of dysentery. Only one study investigated the impact on acute gastroenteritis risk (Mulder et al., 2019). This study found an increased risk of acute gastroenteritis (RR 4.20 [2.10, 8.40]) two weeks post a flood event in the Netherlands. On the other hand, the risks of enteritis caused by VTEC (RR 1.35 [1.25, 1.46]) and cryptosporidiosis (RR 1.57 [1.27, 1.48]) were found to increase a year post a flood event in 2015 summer in Ireland (Boudou et al., 2021). 3.3.2.
