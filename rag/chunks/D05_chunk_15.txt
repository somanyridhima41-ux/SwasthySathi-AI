With all of the confidence intervals included the null value, no significant association was found. The association between floods and the risk of typhoid in one year in a city in Indonesia was not statistically significant (RR 1.65 [0.88, 3.08]); however, there was an increased risk of paratyphoid (RR 4.52 [1.90, 10.73]) (Vollaard et al., 2004). Hand-foot-mouth disease was investigated in one study in mainland China (Hu et al., 2018b), which found an increased risk during 0–1 month post exposure to floods (RR 1.18 [1.03, 1.35]). While the risk of HAV increased (RR 1.40 [1.11, 1.15]) on the 15th day post a flood event in Anhui, mainland China, no significant association was found between this flood event and HEV (RR 0.94 [0.67, 1.31]) (Gao et al., 2016). The risk of malaria incidence was increased after flood events in Sudan, mainland China, and Uganda, although the time frames varied. In Sudan, the incidence risk of malaria during 0–11 week post a flood event was higher than the risk during the same period in the 2 years preceding the event (Elsanousi et al., 2018); in mainland China, the risk reached a peak on the 27th day post a flood event (RR 3.44 [3.28, 3.62])48; and the risk was higher (RR 1,30 [1.16, 1.46]) in flooded villages, compared to non-flooded villages in the year following a flood event in Uganda (Boyce et al., 2016). The impact on Japanese encephalitis was investigated in two studies in mainland China. The authors reported that the smallest and largest risks of Japanese encephalitis occurred on 11th and 24th days post floods in Anhui (RR 0.58 [0.47, 0.71]) (Gao et al., 2016) and Sichuan (2.00 [1.14, 3.52]) (Zhang et al., 2016a), respectively. Only one study estimated the association of floods with human leptospirosis incidence, covering flood events during 2010–2012 in Thailand (Suwanpakdee et al., 2015).
