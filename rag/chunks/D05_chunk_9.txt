Only one study in the UK examined the effect of flood in the ≥6 month time interval, finding that the risk of all-cause mortality decreased in one year (RR 0.90 [0.82, 0.99]) (Milojevic et al., 2011). For specific mortality outcomes (Fig. 4), the risk of CVD mortality was found to increase during 0–4 days post floods (Yan et al., 2020). However, no significant association was found for respiratory mortality (Yan et al., 2020). 3.3. Associations with morbidity The non-external hospitalization risk was found increased (RR 1.07 [1.03, 1.10]) during the four weeks post floods in Vietnam (Phung et al., 2021). Another study focused on the impacts of a flood event in Japan (2018) on population aged >45 years. The authors observed that the risk of nursing home admission was three times higher among those affected by floods compared with those not affected by floods (Fig. 4). (Miyamori et al., 2023) This finding suggested that older people might be more vulnerable to the health impacts of floods. 3.3.1. Four studies estimated the impact on diarrhea diseases incidence in the <6 month time interval (Milojevic et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2022). The meta-analysis of the effect estimates yielded an overall RR of 1.11 (1.04, 1.20), with no evidence of between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p-value of test for heterogeneity = 0.47). Among the studies not included in the meta-analysis, the study in Cambodia reported that the risk of diarrhea increased in the current month and third month post flood periods (Saulnier et al., 2018), and the study in China showed that the risk increased on 11th days post the flood event in 2007 (Gao et al., 2016). Only a study in China estimated the effect of flood in the ≥6 month time interval and found that the risk of diarrhea increased after a flood event in 2016 (Zhang et al., 2019). Seven studies estimated the effects on dysentery in the <6 month time interval (Liu et al., 2016, 2017, 2019; Zhang et al., 2016b; Ni et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2016). Nine effect estimates were extracted because two study provided effect estimates for two locations and two flood events, respectively (Liu et al., 2016, 2017). The meta-analysis yielded an overall RR of 1.38 (1.18, 1.62). Large between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 87%, p-value <0.01); however, all the RRs indicated flood events were associated with an increased risk of dysentery. Only one study investigated the impact on acute gastroenteritis risk (Mulder et al., 2019). This study found an increased risk of acute gastroenteritis (RR 4.20 [2.10, 8.40]) two weeks post a flood event in the Netherlands. On the other hand, the risks of enteritis caused by VTEC (RR 1.35 [1.25, 1.46]) and cryptosporidiosis (RR 1.57 [1.27, 1.48]) were found to increase a year post a flood event in 2015 summer in Ireland (Boudou et al., 2021). 3.3.2. Infectious diseases Ten studies (Fig. 4) investigated the impacts on the risks of infectious diseases.
