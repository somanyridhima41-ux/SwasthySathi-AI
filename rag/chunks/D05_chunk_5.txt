The search strategies are available in Section 1 of Appendix. The references cited in relevant reviews identified in the literature search and publications linked to conference abstracts were also examined. Authors were contacted for additional information. 2.3. Screening and selection Two researchers (ZY and WH) independently screened the titles and abstracts of records identified through the literature search using the PICO Portal (https://www.picoportal.org/). Only records that were excluded by both researchers were excluded at this stage. Full-text of potentially eligible studies were retrieved, and screened independently by two researchers (ZY and WH). Disagreements on eligibility were resolved by consulting a senior researcher (PY). 2.4. Data extraction Data extraction was initially piloted by three researchers (ZY, WH, and PY) on a sample of four studies to ensure consistency of data extraction and to modify the data extraction where required. Following piloting, two researchers (ZY and WH) independently performed the data extraction. From each study, we extracted author names, publication year, outcomes, outcome ascertainment method, exposure assessment method, control group, source of the study population, flood events, study type, time frame of impacts (e.g., infectious morbidity was assumed to change during 0–14 days post exposure to flood), statistical analysis methods (e.g., adjustment for lag and covariates), effect estimates (and their confidence intervals). In studies where effects were reported for multiple time frames (e.g., 0–7 days and 0–14 days post flood exposure), the estimate of the longest time frame (i.e., 0–14 days) was extracted. Discrepancies and uncertainty were resolved in regular meetings of the three researchers (ZY, WH, and PY). 2.5. Assessment of risk of bias Two researchers (ZY and WH) independently assessed the risk of bias (RoB) of each study using the National Institutes of Environmental Health Sciences National Toxicology Program Office of Health Assessment and Translation (OHAT) tool (Rooney et al., 2014). As recommended by developers of the OHAT tool, we tailored all criteria for our topic (see Table S1). Using these criteria, key domains (i.e., exposure assessment, outcome assessment, and confounding bias) and other domains (selection bias, attrition/exclusion bias, and other sources of bias) were judged as being at a definitely low, probably low, probably high, or definitely high RoB. Studies were judged to be at a “definitely high RoB” when they had at least one key domain with definitely high RoB and at least four domains with a “probably high” or “definitely high” RoB. Studies judged to have a “definitely high” RoB were excluded from any synthesis (qualitative or quantitative). Discrepancies and uncertainty were resolved in regular meetings of the three researchers (ZY, WH, and PY). 2.6. Data syntheses To determine whether a conclusion can be reached regarding the association of flood exposure with mortality and morbidity (Russo, 2007), inverse variance-weighted random-effects meta-analysis was used to combine relative risk (RR) estimates when three or more RRs with comparable time frames were available for a particular outcome (Wilker et al., 2023). We combined RR estimates based on two time frames, namely, <6 months and ≥6 months (Zhong et al., 2018). Estimates representing a single day (e.g. 11th day post exposure to the flood) or not measured by RRs were not included in the meta-analysis, but are reported. Odds ratios approximate RRs for rare outcomes (i.e. with event rates <10%) and so were treated as RRs and included in the meta-analyses in this circumstance (Sedgwick, 2014; Grimes and Schulz, 2008). We chose the random-effects model because we expected there would be diversity in population, setting and study design across studies (Borenstein et al., 2010). We used the restricted maximum likelihood estimator for between study variability and the Knapp-Hartung method for confidence intervals (Wilker et al., 2023; Borenstein et al., 2010). We quantified inconsistency using the I2 statistic and formally tested for heterogeneity using Cochran’s Q test. We used forest plots to visually display the effect estimates from studies included in meta-analyses and for those not included in a meta-analysis (i.e., when there fewer than three estimates for a particular outcome and timeframe). We undertook post-hoc sensitivity analyses to examine the impact on the meta-analysis estimates of excluding studies with at least three domains with probably/definitely high RoB and by using fixed-effect models. 3. From the 98 full-text articles retrieved, 50 studies met the inclusion criteria (Table S2); however, 13 were judged to be at a definitely high RoB, and were excluded from the qualitative and quantitative syntheses Z. These studies represented 17 countries/territories in eight sub-regions. Most (26/37) studies were conducted in Asia. More than half (20/37) of the studies only covered flood events within one year (Fig. 2B). Almost all studies were restricted to the impacts within one state/ province (Table 1), except for five estimating the impacts of flood events across Ireland (Boudou et al., 2021), mainland China (Guo et al., 2020), Thailand (Suwanpakdee et al., 2015), Vietnam (Phung et al., 2021), and the United Kingdom (Milojevic et al., 2011), respectively. Time-series study was the most widely used study design (Fig. 2C). 3.1.1. Outcome ascertainments A variety of morbidities were assessed as well as all-cause, cardiovascular (CVD), and respiratory mortality. Except for two studies ascertaining outcomes with standardized tools (Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 2011, 2016), and three through self-report (Mulder et al., 2019; Milojevic et al., 2012; Pradhan et al., 2007), almost all (32/37) studies were based on administrative datasets (Table 1). Among the studies employing an administrative dataset, some (9/32) only covered a part of the general population because the datasets were from a simple source (e.g., datasets of hospital visits, emergency visits, or admissions); however, most (23/32) were based on a comprehensive source (e.g., diseases surveillance network, whole-population insurance database, or mortality registration) and so comprehensively captured the incidences of outcomes in the general population. 3.1.2. Exposure assessments The exposure assessment method varied importantly across studies (Table 1). Almost all studies defined the exposed population as those residing in flooded areas during flooded periods. On the temporal scale, exposure to floods was defined as days, weeks, months, seasons, years, or pregnancy periods, with the flood events based on disaster records (Table 1). Using disaster records or interviews, exposure to floods was defined on a spatial scale as individuals residing in households (Milojevic et al., 2012; Miyamori et al., 2023; Yoshida et al., 2021; Vollaard et al., 2004), communities/village/district/town (Boudou et al., 2021; Suwanpakdee et al., 2015; Milojevic et al., 2011; Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 2011, 2016; Pradhan et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2021a; Elsanousi et al., 2018; Vanasse et al., 2016; Deng et al., 2022), cities (Yan et al., 2020; Hetherington et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2016, 2019, 2020; Zhang et al., 2016a, 2016b, 2019; Hu et al., 2018b; Huang et al., 2016; Wade et al., 2014; Ni et al., 2014; Lan et al., 2022), or provinces with flood events (Guo et al., 2020; Phung et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2011). The data used for these assessments were from sources including official records, insurance certifications, and surveys/interviews. Almost all studies in mainland China determined flooded areas and periods by referring to the records provided by Yearbooks of Meteorological Disasters, where flood periods were defined as days with cumulative rainfall of >80 mm for three consecutive days (Ma et al., 2021). Studies in Japan defined flooded households as those destroyed or whose primary family caregiver was seriously injured or lost jobs due to flood events, which were certified by a government-run insurance system (Miyamori et al., 2023; Yoshida et al., 2021). A study in Canada ascertained flooded areas using water level data from governmental hydraulic records (Vanasse et al., 2016).
