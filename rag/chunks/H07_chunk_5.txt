With the global rise in temperatures and ageing populations, heat-related health risks will continue to increase. Effective, evidence-based interventions, such as tailored heat health action plans that consider individual risk factors, local resources, climatic characteristics and healthcare infrastructure, are crucial to minimise these risks. 1. Introduction The anticipated increase in chronic and acute heat exposure among older adults poses a significant global public health challenge (Falchetta et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2022, Bunker et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2021b, Lee and Kim, 2016). Extreme heat increases mortality and morbidity, particularly in older individuals (Bunker et al., 2016, Faurie et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2021b, Liu et al., 2022, Lee and Kim, 2016, Ciss´e et al., 2022). It also imposes a significant economic burden on healthcare systems, with most healthcare costs attributed to older people (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Older adults account for a large proportion of overall healthcare costs due to their higher prevalence of chronic diseases and healthcare utilisation (Kallestrup-Lamb et al., 2024, OECD 2023, WHO 2025). Age-related comorbidities, medication use, compromised physiological function, and financial constraints place many older adults at greater risk of the negative health effects of heat exposure (Zhang et al., 2016). The global population aged 65 years and older is projected to increase from 761 million in 2021 to about 1.6 billion by 2050 (United Nations, 2023). The Global Burden of Disease Study in 2019, identified cardiovascular diseases, neoplasms, and chronic respiratory diseases, with ischemic heart disease, stroke, COPD, dementia, and lower respiratory infections as top causes of death among those aged 70 and older (GBD 2019 Ageing Collaborators, 2022). These climatic and demographic changes will likely lead to increases in both acute and chronic heat-sensitive diseases, presenting significant challenges to healthcare systems (Bunker et al., 2016, United Nations, 2023, Liu et al., 2021a, Liu et al., 2021b, Liu et al., 2022). Although studies on healthcare costs related to heat exposure are limited, existing literature suggests the economic impact on the healthcare system is substantial (Wondmagegn et al., 2019, Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2021). A 2019 systematic review on heat-attributable healthcare costs encompassed ten studies, but only one specifically addressed healthcare costs among older adults (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Heat-related healthcare expenses include ambulance services, emergency department visits, hospital admissions, medical devices, and diagnostic procedures (Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Wondmagegn et al., 2019, Lin et al., 2012). Estimating these costs offers an economic viewpoint on the burden of heat-related health effects (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Previous reviews have explored disease-specific heat-associated risks, such as cardiovascular diseases (Liu et al., 2022, Cheng et al., 2019), mental health conditions (Liu et al., 2021b, Byun et al., 2024), heat-related illnesses (Faurie et al., 2022), renal diseases (Liu et al., 2021a), diabetes (Song et al., 2021) and respiratory diseases (Cheng et al., 2019, Tran et al., 2025), finding older people to be at risk for both mortality and morbidity. However, few systematic reviews focused specifically on older people, quantifying heat-related morbidity and mortality outcomes. A 2016 meta-analysis showed that for every 1◦C increase in temperature, mortality significantly increased for cardiovascular (3.44%), respiratory (3.60%), and cerebrovascular diseases (1.40%). Similarly, a 1◦C increase in temperature was associated with higher morbidity for respiratory diseases (1.65%), diabetes (1.02%), genitourinary diseases (2.12%), and heat-related illnesses (14.83%) (Bunker et al., 2016). A 2011 meta-analysis indicated that individuals aged 65 and older experienced a 2–5% increase in all-cause mortality for each 1◦C rise in temperature (Yu et al., 2012). However, neither metaanalysis assessed the impact of heatwave exposure, explored climatic characteristics, nor conducted risk of bias (RoB), certainty, or strength of evidence assessments. A more recent meta-analysis in 2024 specifically investigated mental and neurological outcomes in older adults and included both high temperature and heatwave exposures. It reported elevated risks for mental disorders during heat events and applied a RoB assessment (Byun et al., 2024). However, the analysis did not account for differences across climate zones and income classifications. While systematic reviews and meta-analyses have considered the impact of climate variability on these associations, the risk of heat-related mortality and morbidity across different climate zones remains unassessed. Other reviews synthesised previous findings narratively (Åstr¨om et al., 2011, Kaltsatou A, 2018). Moreover, many studies on the relationship between heat and health have been published since. Appendix 1, Tables S2 and S3 provides a systematic identification and mapping of relevant reviews, assessing their scope, methodological characteristics, and the evidence gaps addressed by this review. No previous review has comprehensively synthesised mortality, morbidity, and healthcare cost outcomes in older populations, nor provided stratified analyses by climate zones or demographic subgroups. This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses these gaps by quantifying the effects of heat exposure, including high temperatures and heatwaves, on mortality, morbidity, and healthcare costs among older adults based on available epidemiological evidence. It focuses on specific disease categories according to ICD-10, climate zones, and demographic factors, while also assessing risk of bias, overall certainty, and strength of the evidence. 2. Methods This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Appendix 1, Table S1) (Page et al., 2021). The protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023423311). The primary research question guiding this systematic review and meta-analysis is framed using the PECO structure (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcomes) (Morgan et al., 2018) as follows: Among older adults (P), is exposure to high ambient temperatures or heatwaves (E), associated with increased risk of mortality, morbidity, or healthcare costs (O), compared with cooler reference periods or nonheatwave days (C)? 2.1. Eligibility criteria The inclusion and exclusion criteria for study selection were structured using the PECOS framework (Morgan et al., 2018), as outlined in Table 1 and explained in detail below. 1) Population: Although the United Nations defines older persons as those aged ≥ 60 years, no single age threshold is used consistently across research or policy. In some settings, lower thresholds reflect not only shorter life expectancy but also socio-cultural perceptions of S. Günsche et al. This review included studies using definitions of 60 years and above, as well as two studies from lowand lower-middle-income countries, such as Kenya (≥50 years) and Senegal (≥55 years), where lower thresholds aligned with local demographic and health contexts (United Nations, 2024, Bunker et al., 2016). These were included to avoid data loss, as evidence from these regions is limited. Studies were also included if they stratified their analysis for older adults. 2) Exposure: High ambient temperature and heatwave exposure included shortterm exposure to outdoor heat. Eligible exposure metrics included e.g. minimum, maximum, mean, apparent temperature, heat index, excess heat factor (EHF), and other validated indicators. Exposure could be modelled as continuous (e.g., per 1◦C increase above a reference) or categorical, using thresholds such as temperature percentiles compared to a reference (e.g., 29◦C vs. 15◦C), absolute cut-off values (e.g., >35◦C), or defined heatwave periods (e.g., ≥3 consecutive hot days). Heatwaves were eligible if defined by the study as a period of unusually high temperatures, typically over consecutive days, or based on statistical models such as the EHF (Nairn and Fawcett, 2015). Threshold definitions varied by study but were required to reflect excess heat conditions compared to local climate norms. We excluded studies that only adjusted for temperature as a covariate, lacked an appropriate comparator, or assessed only cold or compared seasons (e.g., summer vs. winter) without analysing temperature or heatwaves as defined exposures. Experimental or simulationbased studies, indoor exposures, and self-reported exposure assessments were also excluded. 3) Comparators: Studies that included cooler reference periods or populations not exposed to high temperature or heatwaves, such as non-heatwave days, lower temperature percentiles, or baseline/ reference temperatures defined by the study. Studies without an appropriate comparator or reference condition were excluded. 4) Outcomes: All-cause and disease-specific mortality and morbidity among older adults during periods of high temperature or heatwaves were included. Morbidity outcomes were eligible if they were linked to healthcare utilisation, such as hospital admissions, emergency department visits, ambulance call-outs, or outpatient visits. Healthcare cost outcomes were included when directly related to healthcare utilization during heat exposure. Disease classifications followed the ICD-10 system (codes A00–Z99) and encompassed heat-sensitive categories such as cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, genitourinary, metabolic, and heat-related illnesses Studies were included if they reported outcomes on a daily resolution and provided effect estimates such as relative risk, odds ratio, percent change, or incidence rate ratios with a 95% confidence interval (CI) based on empirical epidemiological data from observed historical health and temperature records. We excluded studies reporting only self-reported symptoms, functional performance, or subjective wellbeing, as well as those focusing solely on physiological parameters or biomarkers without associated health outcomes. Studies using simulated, projected, or scenario-based data were excluded. Studies reporting indirect, productivity, or mortality costs (e.g., value-of-statistical-life estimations or economic valuations of years of life lost) were also excluded. 5) Study designs: Eligible studies used observational ecological study designs such as time series, case crossover, or case time-series designs to compare risks over different exposures or time periods. Only peerreviewed, original studies in English were included. We excluded studies based on self-reported or questionnaire-based exposure or outcome assessment, as well as experimental, simulation-based, or laboratory studies. Also excluded were reviews, editorials, commentaries, theses, conference abstracts, and non-English publications. 2.2.
