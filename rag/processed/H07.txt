ABSTRACT
Background: Exposure to heat extremes is associated with higher rates of mortality, morbidity and healthcare 
costs. In the context of population ageing and climate change, which is expected to make extreme heat events 
more intense, frequent, prolonged, and widespread, understanding their health impacts on older adults is crucial 
for informing targeted interventions.
Methods: This systematic review and meta-analysis searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, 
and AgeLine for studies published between January 1, 1990, and March 15, 2025. Original research articles 
assessing morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs among older adults during periods of high temperatures or 
heatwaves were included. Eligible studies used observational designs, such as time series, case-crossover, or case 
time-series, comparing risks across different exposures or time periods. Reviews, commentaries, grey literature, 
animal studies, and studies not focused on temperature as the primary exposure were excluded. Risk estimates 
were extracted from published studies. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to pool relative risks of 
morbidity and mortality, focusing on specific disease categories according to ICD-10 codes, climate zones, and 
demographic factors. The certainty and strength of the evidence were assessed following the Navigation Guide 
systematic review methodology framework. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO 
(CRD42023423311).
Results: Out of 21,610 identified studies, 623 were eligible, spanning six continents and 305 locations classified 
by K¨oppen climate zones. Of these, 578 were included in the meta-analysis, 43 in a supplementary meta-analysis, 
and four on healthcare costs were narratively synthesised. A 1◦C temperature rise was associated with higher 
morbidity (RR 1.016 [CI95% 1.012–1.019]) and mortality (RR 1.022 [CI95% 1.020–1.024]) among older people. 
The highest morbidity risks were linked to heat-related illnesses, external causes and endocrine diseases, and in 
tropical climates. Mortality risk was highest for intentional self-harm and external causes, chronic obstructive 
pulmonary disease (COPD), and in Mediterranean climates, with the strongest overall impact on those aged ≥ 85 
years. Heatwaves increased morbidity (RR 1.189 [CI95% 1.146–1.233]), particularly for heat-related illnesses 
and external causes, with the highest overall risk seen in adults aged 75–84 and in subtropical climates. Mortality 
also rose (RR 1.117 [CI95% 1.105–1.129]), especially for cerebrovascular, endocrine and respiratory diseases, 
with the greatest overall risks found in tropical climates and among individuals aged ≥ 85 years. Despite the 
limited number of studies on healthcare costs they showed significant increases in costs during heat exposure for 
emergency department presentations, respiratory and ureter calculus hospitalisations. There was high hetero-
geneity with morbidity (high temperatures: I2 = 84.1%; heatwaves: I2 = 97.6%), whilst for mortality, there was

global rise in temperatures and ageing populations, heat-related health risks will continue to increase. Effective,

resources, climatic characteristics and healthcare infrastructure, are crucial to minimise these risks.
1. Introduction
The anticipated increase in chronic and acute heat exposure among 
older adults poses a significant global public health challenge (Falchetta 
et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2022, Bunker et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2021b, Lee 
and Kim, 2016). Extreme heat increases mortality and morbidity, 
particularly in older individuals (Bunker et al., 2016, Faurie et al., 2022, 
Liu et al., 2021b, Liu et al., 2022, Lee and Kim, 2016, Ciss´e et al., 2022). 
It also imposes a significant economic burden on healthcare systems, 
with most healthcare costs attributed to older people (Wondmagegn 
et al., 2019). Older adults account for a large proportion of overall 
healthcare costs due to their higher prevalence of chronic diseases and 
healthcare utilisation (Kallestrup-Lamb et al., 2024, OECD 2023, WHO 
2025). Age-related comorbidities, medication use, compromised physi-
ological function, and financial constraints place many older adults at 
greater risk of the negative health effects of heat exposure (Zhang et al., 
2016).
The global population aged 65 years and older is projected to in-
crease from 761 million in 2021 to about 1.6 billion by 2050 (United 
Nations, 2023). The Global Burden of Disease Study in 2019, identified 
cardiovascular diseases, neoplasms, and chronic respiratory diseases, 
with ischemic heart disease, stroke, COPD, dementia, and lower respi-
ratory infections as top causes of death among those aged 70 and older 
(GBD 2019 Ageing Collaborators, 2022). These climatic and de-
mographic changes will likely lead to increases in both acute and 
chronic heat-sensitive diseases, presenting significant challenges to 
healthcare systems (Bunker et al., 2016, United Nations, 2023, Liu et al., 
2021a, Liu et al., 2021b, Liu et al., 2022).
Although studies on healthcare costs related to heat exposure are 
limited, existing literature suggests the economic impact on the 
healthcare system is substantial (Wondmagegn et al., 2019, Wondma-
gegn et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2021).
A 2019 systematic review on heat-attributable healthcare costs 
encompassed ten studies, but only one specifically addressed healthcare 
costs among older adults (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Heat-related 
healthcare expenses include ambulance services, emergency depart-
ment visits, hospital admissions, medical devices, and diagnostic pro-
cedures (Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Wondmagegn et al., 2019, Lin et al., 
2012). Estimating these costs offers an economic viewpoint on the 
burden of heat-related health effects (Wondmagegn et al., 2019).
Previous reviews have explored disease-specific heat-associated 
risks, such as cardiovascular diseases (Liu et al., 2022, Cheng et al., 
2019), mental health conditions (Liu et al., 2021b, Byun et al., 2024), 
heat-related illnesses (Faurie et al., 2022), renal diseases (Liu et al., 
2021a), diabetes (Song et al., 2021) and respiratory diseases (Cheng 
et al., 2019, Tran et al., 2025), finding older people to be at risk for both 
mortality and morbidity. However, few systematic reviews focused 
specifically on older people, quantifying heat-related morbidity and 
mortality outcomes. A 2016 meta-analysis showed that for every 1◦C 
increase in temperature, mortality significantly increased for cardio-
vascular (3.44%), respiratory (3.60%), and cerebrovascular diseases 
(1.40%). Similarly, a 1◦C increase in temperature was associated with 
higher morbidity for respiratory diseases (1.65%), diabetes (1.02%), 
genitourinary diseases (2.12%), and heat-related illnesses (14.83%) 
(Bunker et al., 2016). A 2011 meta-analysis indicated that individuals 
aged 65 and older experienced a 2–5% increase in all-cause mortality for 
each 1◦C rise in temperature (Yu et al., 2012). However, neither meta- 
analysis assessed the impact of heatwave exposure, explored climatic 
characteristics, nor conducted risk of bias (RoB), certainty, or strength of 
evidence assessments.
A more recent meta-analysis in 2024 specifically investigated mental 
and neurological outcomes in older adults and included both high 
temperature and heatwave exposures. It reported elevated risks for 
mental disorders during heat events and applied a RoB assessment (Byun 
et al., 2024). However, the analysis did not account for differences 
across climate zones and income classifications. While systematic re-
views and meta-analyses have considered the impact of climate vari-
ability on these associations, the risk of heat-related mortality and 
morbidity across different climate zones remains unassessed. Other re-
views synthesised previous findings narratively (Åstr¨om et al., 2011, 
Kaltsatou A, 2018). Moreover, many studies on the relationship between 
heat and health have been published since.
Appendix 1, Tables S2 and S3 provides a systematic identification 
and mapping of relevant reviews, assessing their scope, methodological 
characteristics, and the evidence gaps addressed by this review. No 
previous review has comprehensively synthesised mortality, morbidity, 
and healthcare cost outcomes in older populations, nor provided strat-
ified analyses by climate zones or demographic subgroups.
This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses these gaps by 
quantifying the effects of heat exposure, including high temperatures 
and heatwaves, on mortality, morbidity, and healthcare costs among 
older adults based on available epidemiological evidence. It focuses on 
specific disease categories according to ICD-10, climate zones, and de-
mographic factors, while also assessing risk of bias, overall certainty, 
and strength of the evidence.
2. Methods
This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred 
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 
guidelines (Appendix 1, Table S1) (Page et al., 2021). The protocol for 
this review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023423311).
The primary research question guiding this systematic review and 
meta-analysis is framed using the PECO structure (Population, Exposure, 
Comparator, Outcomes) (Morgan et al., 2018) as follows:
Among older adults (P), is exposure to high ambient temperatures or 
heatwaves (E), associated with increased risk of mortality, morbidity, or 
healthcare costs (O), compared with cooler reference periods or non- 
heatwave days (C)?
2.1. Eligibility criteria
The inclusion and exclusion criteria for study selection were struc-
tured using the PECOS framework (Morgan et al., 2018), as outlined in 
Table 1 and explained in detail below. 
1) Population: Although the United Nations defines older persons as 
those aged ≥ 60 years, no single age threshold is used consistently 
across research or policy. In some settings, lower thresholds reflect 
not only shorter life expectancy but also socio-cultural perceptions of

ageing (United Nations, 2024). This review included studies using 
definitions of 60 years and above, as well as two studies from low- 
and lower-middle-income countries, such as Kenya (≥50 years) and 
Senegal (≥55 years), where lower thresholds aligned with local de-
mographic and health contexts (United Nations, 2024, Bunker et al., 
2016). These were included to avoid data loss, as evidence from 
these regions is limited. Studies were also included if they stratified 
their analysis for older adults.
2) Exposure: High ambient temperature and heatwave exposure 
included short- term exposure to outdoor heat. Eligible exposure 
metrics included e.g. minimum, maximum, mean, apparent tem-
perature, heat index, excess heat factor (EHF), and other validated 
indicators. Exposure could be modelled as continuous (e.g., per 1◦C 
increase above a reference) or categorical, using thresholds such as 
temperature percentiles compared to a reference (e.g., 29◦C vs. 
15◦C), absolute cut-off values (e.g., >35◦C), or defined heatwave 
periods (e.g., ≥3 consecutive hot days).
Heatwaves were eligible if defined by the study as a period of un-
usually high temperatures, typically over consecutive days, or based on 
statistical models such as the EHF (Nairn and Fawcett, 2015). Threshold 
definitions varied by study but were required to reflect excess heat 
conditions compared to local climate norms.
We excluded studies that only adjusted for temperature as a covar-
iate, lacked an appropriate comparator, or assessed only cold or 
compared seasons (e.g., summer vs. winter) without analysing temper-
ature or heatwaves as defined exposures. Experimental or simulation- 
based studies, indoor exposures, and self-reported exposure assess-
ments were also excluded. 
3) Comparators: Studies that included cooler reference periods or 
populations not exposed to high temperature or heatwaves, such as 
non-heatwave days, lower temperature percentiles, or baseline/ 
reference temperatures defined by the study. Studies without an 
appropriate comparator or reference condition were excluded.
4) Outcomes: All-cause and disease-specific mortality and morbidity 
among older adults during periods of high temperature or heatwaves 
were included. Morbidity outcomes were eligible if they were linked 
to healthcare utilisation, such as hospital admissions, emergency 
department visits, ambulance call-outs, or outpatient visits. Health-
care cost outcomes were included when directly related to healthcare 
utilization during heat exposure. Disease classifications followed the 
ICD-10 system (codes A00–Z99) and encompassed heat-sensitive 
categories such as cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, genitourinary, 
metabolic, and heat-related illnesses Studies were included if they 
reported outcomes on a daily resolution and provided effect esti-
mates such as relative risk, odds ratio, percent change, or incidence 
rate ratios with a 95% confidence interval (CI) based on empirical 
epidemiological data from observed historical health and tempera-
ture records. We excluded studies reporting only self-reported 
symptoms, functional performance, or subjective wellbeing, as well 
as those focusing solely on physiological parameters or biomarkers 
without associated health outcomes. Studies using simulated, pro-
jected, or scenario-based data were excluded. Studies reporting in-
direct, productivity, or mortality costs (e.g., value-of-statistical-life 
estimations or economic valuations of years of life lost) were also 
excluded.
5) Study designs: Eligible studies used observational ecological study 
designs such as time series, case crossover, or case time-series designs 
to compare risks over different exposures or time periods. Only peer- 
reviewed, original studies in English were included. We excluded 
studies based on self-reported or questionnaire-based exposure or 
outcome assessment, as well as experimental, simulation-based, or 
laboratory studies. Also excluded were reviews, editorials, com-
mentaries, theses, conference abstracts, and non-English 
publications.
2.2. Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted with guidance 
from the university librarian across six databases: PubMed, Embase, 
Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and AgeLine. The search strategy was 
structured around the PECO framework and applied keywords and 
Boolean operators covering the following domains (full search terms 
listed in Appendix 1, Table S4):
Population (e.g., “Older people”, “Aged”), Exposure/Comparator (e. 
g., “Heat”, “Hot weather”, “High Temperature”), Outcomes (e.g., 
“Health outcomes”, “Morbidity”, “Mortality”, “Health care cost”).
Within each domain, search terms were combined using “OR” to 
include all relevant variants. These groups were then connected using 
the “AND” operator.
Pilot testing of the search strategy was conducted during its devel-
opment. Based on preliminary findings, outcome-related terms were 
refined to include specific morbidity indicators such as hospitalisation, 
ambulance call-out, emergency department visits, and outpatient visits.
To assess the completeness and validity of the search, we reviewed 
the reference lists of 25 relevant systematic reviews identified through 
targeted searches (Appendix 1, pp. S3–S12). This step helped identify 
additional eligible primary studies not retrieved through the initial 
search and confirmed adequate conceptual coverage.
The final search was conducted for studies published between 
January 1, 1990, and May 31, 2023. An updated search was performed 
on March 15, 2025, to capture newly published literature.

2.4. Risk of bias assessment
The RoB for individual studies was assessed using a modified version 
of the Office of Health Assessment and Translation (OHAT) RoB Tool, 
following the Navigation Guide for systematic reviews in environmental 
health (OHAT, 2019, Liu et al., 2022, Johnson et al., 2014). Each study 
was evaluated across eight domains: exposure assessment, outcome 
assessment, confounding, selection bias, incomplete or missing data, 
selective reporting, conflict of interest, and other sources of bias. For 
each domain, risk was rated as “low,” “probably low,” “probably high,” 
or “high” risk based on pre-specified criteria (Appendix 1, Table S5). The 
intermediate categories (“probably low” and “probably high”) were 
applied when information was insufficient for a definitive judgment but 
provided indirect evidence toward lower or higher risk, respectively, 
consistent with Navigation Guide conventions. The overall RoB was 
determined based on three key domains: exposure, outcome, and con-
founding (Liu et al., 2022). The rationale for overall bias ratings is 
provided in Appendix 1, Table S6.
Six reviewers (SG, YL, MB, JLi, YH, YS) independently conducted 
RoB assessments and resolved disagreements through discussion.
2.5. Synthesis of the evidence
Study findings were synthesised both quantitatively and narratively. 
A DerSimonian and Laird random-effects meta-analysis was conducted 
(DerSimonian and Laird, 1986) to pool effect estimates from studies that 
provided relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), incidence rate ratio (IRR), 
or other effect estimates that could be converted to RR (e.g. percentage 
change or excess risk) (DerSimonian and Laird, 1986).
For high temperature exposures, estimates were converted to RR per 
1◦C increase above the study-specific reference temperature when 
studies reported temperature contrasts or increments other than 1◦C 
(Liu et al., 2022). This standardisation/conversion assumed a log‑linear 
relationship within the high‑temperature range and was used as a sta-
tistical scaling to improve comparability across studies, recognising that 
a 1◦C increase in different metrics does not necessarily represent the 
same underlying physical change in thermal exposure.
Separate meta-analyses were performed for heatwave studies, pool-
ing RRs of outcomes during heatwave versus non– heatwave periods. 
Heatwave definitions varied widely across studies in terms of tempera-
ture thresholds and durations. To account for this variability, subgroup 
analyses were performed by heatwave intensity   low (90th–93rd 
percentile), middle (94th–96th percentile), and high (97th–99th 
percentile) (Liu et al., 2022)   as well as durations (e.g. ≥ 2 days, ≥3 
days, ≥4 days).
Studies reporting high temperature exposure using categories or 
percentiles only (e.g. 95th vs. 50th percentile, or 99th (34◦C) vs. refer-
ence temperature) without reporting the corresponding absolute tem-
perature were included in a secondary meta-analysis (Appendix 1, 
Tables S16 and S17) in which effect estimates were pooled as reported.
Subgroup analyses were performed when four or more estimates 
were available to explore variations in disease outcomes, demographic 
factors, national income levels, K¨oppen-Geiger climate zones (Peel et al., 
2007), continents and healthcare utilisation outcomes. Disease out-
comes were categorised using the International Classification of Dis-
eases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10) nomenclature and hierarchy.
For the studies that reported multiple estimates for either individual 
lag days (e.g. lag 0, 1, 2) or age groups (e.g. 60–85, ≥ 85 years) without 
providing an overall estimate, these estimates were combined into a 
single study-specific effect using an inverse-variance fixed-effect meta- 
analysis. Effect estimates were first transformed to the log scale and 
weighted by the inverse of their variance to obtain one overall estimate 
per study for inclusion in the main analysis (Liu et al., 2022, Liu et al., 
2021b, Byun et al., 2024, Higgins et al., 2024). Multi-location studies 
included estimates from all locations for pooled effects (Liu et al., 2022).
For outcomes that could not be statistically pooled (i.e., health care 
costs), a narrative synthesis was performed. This included a structured 
summary of the direction, magnitude, and precision of reported effect 
estimates, in line with recommendations (Popay et al., 2005).
Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane's Q and Higgins’ I2 sta-
tistics, with I2 values categorised as low (0–25%), moderate (26–74%), 
and high (≥75%) (Higgins et al., 2003). Additionally, 80% prediction 
intervals (PI) were used to estimate the range within which the true RR 
for future studies is likely to fall, with severe heterogeneity indicated if 
the 80% PI included the null effect and was more than twice the width of 
the 95% CIs (IntHout et al., 2016).
Publication bias was evaluated using Egger's test, funnel plots (Egger 
et al., 1997), and the trim-and-fill method (Duval and Tweedie, 2004). A 
univariate meta-regression analysis was performed to explore study- 
level characteristics that may explain between-study heterogeneity in 
effect sizes (Liu et al., 2022, Bown and Sutton, 2010, Deeks et al., 2024). 
The variables tested included K¨oppen-Geiger climate group, lag effects, 
age group, sex, continent, hemisphere, heatwave intensity and duration, 
and income classification.
Sensitivity analyses were undertaken by categorising studies ac-
cording to temperature metrics, study design, lag periods, overall RoB 
rating, seasonal variations (warm season vs. whole year), air pollution 
and humidity adjustment, as well as modelling approach (non-linear or 
linear) (Liu et al., 2022). Leave-one-out analyses were used to assess the 
impact of individual studies on pooled RRs.
Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.1) 
(R Core Team, 2024) with the package metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010).
2.6. Certainty of the evidence
The certainty of the evidence was assessed following the Navigation 
Guide for systematic reviews of observational studies in environmental 
health (Johnson et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2016). This process involved 
three steps, with the first step—Risk of Bias (RoB) asses-
sment—described in Section 2.4.
2) The certainty of evidence across studies was assessed using the 
Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evalua-
tion (GRADE) framework (Guyatt et al., 2011). Evidence for each 
exposure–outcome pair was initially rated as “moderate” and could be 
downgraded for domains such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirect-
ness, imprecision, and publication bias. It could be upgraded based on 
evidence for a dose–response relationship, large magnitude of effect, or 
residual confounding that would likely attenuate the observed

association. The final certainty rating was assigned as “high”, “moder-
ate”, or “low”, based on the net impact of downgrading and upgrading 
(Johnson et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2016). The decision rules and 
justifications for these adjustments are provided in Appendix 1, 
Table S7, which are also described in detail elsewhere (Johnson et al., 
2014, Johnson et al., 2016).
3) The overall strength of the evidence was determined by combining 
certainty assessments with consistency of findings and confidence in 
estimated effects. Evidence was categorized as “sufficient,” “limited,” or 
“inadequate,” following the Navigation Guide framework (Johnson 
et al., 2014, Johnson et al., 2016).
3. Results
3.1. Study selection and characteristics
The database searches retrieved 21,610 records. After filtering non- 
English, non-human or non-journal articles and removing duplicates 
12,131 records remained for title and abstract screening. Most records 
were excluded at this stage as they were unrelated to heat exposure and 
health outcomes, were the wrong publication type or not based on 
human data. Hence, 812 studies were assessed in full text. A further 210 
studies were excluded, primarily due to irrelevant outcomes or expo-
sures, unsuitable study design, or populations outside the inclusion 
criteria (see Fig. 1 for details). An additional 21 studies were identified 
through the screening of 25 relevant systematic reviews, which had been 
retrieved via database searches and included data on older adults, sug-
gesting the primary search was comprehensive. In total, 623 studies met 
the inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1).
Table 2 provides an overview of the 623 included studies, cat-
egorised by exposure type and reported outcome.
A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted for morbidity and 
mortality outcomes in relation to high temperature and heatwave 
exposure (n = 578). An additional 43 studies, which reported high 
temperature effects using non-standardizable exposure metrics (e.g. 
high temperature vs. study-defined reference such as minimum risk or 
percentile-based values), were synthesised in a supplementary meta- 
analysis (Appendix 1, Tables S16 and S17).
A meta-analysis for healthcare costs was not feasible due to the 
limited number of eligible studies. Four studies reported healthcare 
costs; two of them also contributed to the meta-analysis as they reported 
morbidity and heat exposure outcomes alongside cost estimates, while 
two were included in the narrative synthesis only. Key findings from all 
included studies are summarised in the supplementary material
(Appendix 2).
The included studies were published between 1995 and 2025 and 
were conducted across Asia, Africa, Australia, Europe, North America, 
and South America. There were 154 location-specific studies for mor-
tality and 151 for morbidity, which could be classified by the K¨oppen- 
Geiger climate zones: A (tropical), B (arid), C (temperate–including 
Mediterranean, Oceanic, and subtropical climates), D (continental), and 
E (polar) (Peel et al., 2007). Most studies were conducted in temperate 
(C) and continental (D) climates, primarily across North America, 
Europe, and East Asia (Fig. 2). A complete list of all study countries and 
locations is provided in Appendix 1, Table S8.
3.2. Risk of bias
The risk of bias assessment was low for 159 studies (27.4%), prob-
ably low for 380 studies (65.5%), probably high for 40 studies (6.9%), 
and high for 1 study (0.2%).

3.3. Synthesis of high temperature effects
Overall, a 1◦C rise in temperature increased morbidity (RR 1.016 
[CI95% 1.012–1.019]) (Fig. 4) and mortality (RR 1.022 [CI95% 
1.020–1.024]) among older individuals (Fig. 5). The highest morbidity 
risks were linked to heat-related illnesses (RR 1.228 [CI95% 
1.155–1.307]), external causes (RR 1.109 [CI95% 1.071–1.149]), and 
endocrine diseases (RR 1.035 [CI95% 1.010–1.059]), while cardiovas-
cular, digestive, infectious, and mental diseases showed no significant 
associations. Individuals aged ≥ 85 years, those in tropical climates, 
lower-middle-income countries, and residents of Asia experienced the 
largest increases in morbidity per 1◦C rise (Fig. 4).
Mortality risk was mostly elevated for intentional self-harm (RR 
1.068 [CI95% 1.026–1.113]), external causes (RR 1.039 [CI95% 
1.016–1.063]), COPD (RR 1.038 [CI95% 1.001–1.076]), cerebrovascu-
lar (RR 1.031 [CI95% 1.012–1.049]), respiratory (RR 1.029 [CI95% 
1.019–1.039]) and cardiovascular diseases (RR 1.021 [CI95% 
1.014–1.027]) (Fig. 5).
The overall test of heterogeneity was statistically significant for 
continent (QM (df = 5) = 24.8609, p = 0.0001, I2 = 0.0%), age group 
(QM (df = 2) = 11.7780, p = 0.0028, I2 = 0.0%), and hemisphere (QM 
(df = 1) = 11.3437, p = 0.0008), suggesting that these factors may 
significantly modify the association between high temperatures and 
mortality. Among continents, Australia showed the strongest increase in 
mortality (RR 1.075 [95% CI 1.052–1.098], p < 0.0001), followed by 
Europe (RR 1.019 [95% CI 1.012–1.027], p < 0.0001) Asia (RR 1.010 
[95% CI 1.002–1.018], p = 0.0129), and North America (RR 1.009 [95% 
CI 1.002–1.015], p = 0.0069).
Age-stratified effects showed significantly increased mortality for the 
reference group aged 60–74 years (RR 1.015 [95% CI 1.009–1.022], p <
0.0001) and those aged ≥ 85 years (RR 1.018 [95% CI 1.008–1.029], p 
= 0.0006).
Hemispheres showed significantly elevated mortality risks. The 
Northern Hemisphere (reference) had an effect estimate of RR 1.021 
[95% CI 1.018–1.024], p < 0.0001, while the Southern Hemisphere 
showed a comparable effect (RR 1.021 [95% CI 1.009–1.033], p =
0.0008) (Appendix 1, Table S9).

in emergency department visits and large attributable costs among

4.2. Context with previous evidence
The health impacts of extreme heat on older adults are widely rec-
ognised, yet age-specific quantitative syntheses remain scarce. Only few 
systematic reviews have specifically concentrated on older adults, 
providing quantitative analyses of heat-related disease and mortality 
outcomes. Bunker et al. undertaken in 2016, is the only systematic re-
view to date that meta-analysed 37 studies to quantify cause-specific 
heat effects on both mortality and morbidity across multiple disease 
categories in older populations. They found that per 1◦C increase in 
temperature, mortality increased by 3.44% (95% CI: 3.10–3.78) for 
cardiovascular, 3.60% (95% CI: 3.18–4.02) for respiratory, and 1.40% 
(95% CI: 0.06–2.75) for cerebrovascular diseases. For morbidity, the 
largest increases were observed for heat-related illnesses (14.83%, 95% 
CI: 8.22–21.84), respiratory diseases (2.76%, 95% CI: 1.51–4.03), dia-
betes (1.02%, 95% CI: 0.43–1.62), and acute renal failure (2.12%, 95% 
CI: 1.59–2.65). However, the effects of heatwaves were not investigated, 
nor were risk of bias evaluations or climatic and geographic variability 
addressed.
A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Byun et al. (2024)
synthesised evidence on high temperatures and heatwaves in relation to 
mental and neurological outcomes, including studies with older pop-
ulations. Their meta-analysis of five studies found that each 1◦C increase 
in temperature was associated with an increase in hospital visits for 
mental disorders (RR: 1.014, 95% CI: 1.001–1.026). For heatwaves, the 
pooled effect estimates showed a rise in hospital visits (RR: 1.269, 95% 
CI: 1.030–1.564), while mortality from mental disorders showed a 
similar pattern (RR: 1.266, 95% CI: 0.956–1.678), although this was not 
statistically significant. The review also identified substantial method-
ological heterogeneity across studies, especially regarding heatwave 
definitions and temperature thresholds.
Furthermore, a 2012 meta-analysis estimated a 2–5% rise in all-cause 
mortality per 1◦C among those ≥ 65 years (Yu et al., 2012) and narrative 
reviews have reported consistent heat-wave–related increases in car-
diovascular and respiratory mortality (Åstr¨om et al., 2011, Kaltsatou A, 
2018).
Our updated findings are broadly consistent with previous system-
atic reviews and show similar magnitudes for key outcomes in older 
populations, particularly for cardiovascular, respiratory, and cerebro-
vascular conditions. Notably, we also identified additional cause- 
specific risks, including e.g. external causes, intentional self-harm, 
digestive diseases, COPD, asthma, and dementia/Alzheimer’s disease, 
that were not previously synthesised. These likely reflect both the 
expansion in the available evidence and a growing recognition of non- 
traditional heat-related health outcomes.
Overall, our pooled estimate of a 2.2% (95% CI: 2–2.4) increase in 
mortality per 1◦C rise in temperature falls within the 2–5% range re-
ported by Yu et al (2012).
These differences in outcomes may be explained by the substantially 
larger number of included studies, many using recent multi-city datasets 
with adaptive threshold modelling and the inclusion of a broader, more 
up-to-date evidence base. This reflects the expanded scope of available 
research and captures a wider spectrum of heat-related morbidity and 
mortality, including more recent and previously underrepresented 
health outcomes.
Despite growing evidence on the health risks of heat, studies quan-
tifying the economic burden remain sparse. A 2019 systematic review 
identified only 10 studies on heat-related healthcare costs, with just one 
focusing specifically on older adults (Wondmagegn et al., 2019). Re-
ported costs included ambulance services, emergency department visits, 
hospital admissions, medical devices, and diagnostic procedures 
(Wondmagegn et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2024, Wang 
et al., 2021). These data suggest that the economic impact of extreme 
heat on healthcare systems is substantial yet under-investigated in older 
populations.
Our review identified only three studies quantifying healthcare costs 
in the context of heat, all reporting significant increases in expenses 
related to respiratory admissions and emergency visits. However, there 
is still no comprehensive synthesis that quantifies heat-attributable costs 
by disease type, age group, or climate zone. This underscores the need 
for more targeted economic evaluations, particularly as the ageing 
population continues to grow and climate-related health risks intensify.
4.3. Subgroup analysis and potential underlying mechanism
External causes of morbidity, particularly heat-related illnesses such 
as heatstroke, heat exhaustion, and dehydration, significantly increased 
during heatwaves, confirming previous findings (Faurie et al., 2022, 
Bunker et al., 2016). Prolonged exposure to high temperatures can 
overwhelm the body’s ability to cool itself, leading to fluid and salt loss 
and symptoms like nausea, confusion, and seizures. If untreated, this can 
result in multi-organ failure (Hifumi et al., 2018). Older adults, with 
diminished thirst perception and widespread use of medications like 
diuretics, are at greater risk of dehydration (Meade et al., 2020).
CVDs, particularly cerebrovascular conditions, were strongly asso-
ciated with heat exposure and mortality. This finding was in line with 
previous studies (Bunker et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2022, Cheng et al., 2019) 
and could be explained by heat exposure increasing skin blood flow and 
sweating, leading to volume depletion, sympathetic nervous system 
activation, and elevated heart rate (Crandall and Wilson, 2015). This is 
particularly challenging for older adults, as their physiological ther-
moregulatory capacity decreases with age. Specifically, sweat gland 
output becomes less effective, blood flow to the skin is reduced, and 
increases in cardiac output are smaller (Meade et al., 2020). This raises 
metabolic demand, potentially causing ischemia or thrombosis (Peters 
and Schneider, 2021). Fluid loss and electrolyte imbalances further in-
crease the risk of arrhythmias and myocardial ischemia (Liu et al., 
2015).. Older people are also more likely to take medications that can 
impair thermoregulatory responses such as antihypertensives, or di-
uretics that increase water excretion and thus predispose to dehydra-
tion. These medications are linked to increased mortality during higher 
temperatures (Ebi et al., 2021).
The higher CVD mortality compared to morbidity may result from 
several factors, including the possibility that patients with severe CVD 
die before receiving medical care. Limited access to healthcare and cases 
that do not result in hospitalisation or emergency department visits can 
contribute to underreporting of morbidity, especially during heat pe-
riods (Lee et al., 2024, Michelozzi et al., 2009). Additionally, older 
adults face heightened risk due to multiple health conditions, increased

physiological fragility, and difficulties accessing care on their own (Lee 
et al., 2024, Leon and Helwig, 2010). To better capture the effects of 
temperature on morbidity, data from outpatient settings, such as general 
practices and home visits, should be included, as they are currently 
underrepresented (Bunker et al., 2016, Smith et al., 2016).
Respiratory mortality and morbidity were significantly elevated, 
consistent with previous studies (Achebak et al., 2024, Bunker et al., 
2016). The mechanisms leading to adverse respiratory outcomes are not 
fully understood but are likely influenced by thermoregulatory path-
ways (Michelozzi et al., 2009, Achebak et al., 2024). Heat exposure can 
worsen conditions like COPD by increasing ventilation and inflamma-
tory responses (Viegi et al., 2007, Anderson et al., 2013, White, 2006, 
Leon and Helwig, 2010) COPD is a common medical condition among 
older people (Bunker et al., 2016, Mollica et al., 2021). This may lead to 
hyperventilation during extreme heat, increasing the risk of hyperin-
flation, dyspnea, and cardiovascular complications (Sprung, 1980). 
Inhalation of hot air can also trigger adverse airway reactions (Anderson 
et al., 2013) and cause bronchoconstriction in patients with asthma 
(Hayes et al., 2012). However, this study found a significant effect for 
COPD morality per 1 degree increase but not for morbidity. No signifi-
cant effects were found for asthma, or pneumonia, though the overall 
respiratory impact was notable across all exposures and outcomes.
Endocrine diseases increased with heat exposure, with diabetes 
showing significant associations with heatwaves and mortality. Previous 
reviews also found diabetes significantly elevated among older adults 
during heat exposure (Bunker et al., 2016, Song et al., 2021).
Diabetes is linked to various metabolic, cardiovascular, and neuro-
logical dysfunctions, which can also impact thermoregulatory mecha-
nisms during heat exposure (Kenny et al., 2010). In both type 1 and type 
2 diabetes, the blood vessels in the skin may have reduced ability to 
dilate, potentially leading to decreased blood flow to the skin's surface 
and impairing heat dissipation (Petrofsky et al., 2005, Stansberry et al., 
1997, Wick et al., 2006).
Heat exposure is significantly related with kidney and urinary dis-
eases mortality and morbidity. During further subgroup analysis, renal 
failure morbidity was observed to be an increased risk among older 
people. The kidneys play a key role in thermoregulation by regulating 
blood pressure and maintaining fluid and electrolyte balances (Robson, 
2014). Dehydration and reduced blood flow during overheating increase 
the risk of kidney damage. Ageing kidneys take longer to correct im-
balances under heat stress (de Lorenzo and Lia˜no, 2017, Rivas Velasquez 
et al., 2014).
Morbidity due to mental and behavioural disorders was significantly 
elevated during heatwaves, while no significant associations were 
observed for mortality from heatwaves or morbidity during periods of 
high temperature. Importantly, a notable increase in mortality from 
intentional self-harm during high temperatures was identified, further 
highlighting the mental health burden of extreme temperatures. These 
findings align with previous meta-analytic evidence reporting increased 
risks of heat-related morbidity and mortality due to mental health 
conditions among older adults (Liu et al., 2021b). Age-related factors, 
along with psychotropic medications, impair the body’s ability to 
manage heat and increase the risk of confusion, delirium, and cognitive 
decline (Yezli, 2023, Maes et al., 2023). Heat exposure can also intensify 
psychological distress and trigger risk behaviours, including alcohol use, 
substance abuse, and suicidal tendencies, especially when individuals 
are unable to find relief from the heat (Liu et al., 2021b, Page et al., 
2007).
While the pooled estimate for dementia and Alzheimer’s disease 
during high temperatures was not statistically significant, the number of 
available estimates was limited. This may change as more studies 
emerge, given the high prevalence of these neurodegenerative condi-
tions in older adults and their known vulnerability to heat.
People aged ≥ 85 years had the highest risk overall, but for heat-
waves and morbidity, the 75–84 age group showed a dramatically 
higher risk (116.8%, 95% CI: 24.2–278.4) compared to those aged 
60–64 (34.2%, 95% CI: 21.2–48.6) and ≥ 85 years (28.9%, 95% CI: 
12.7–47.4). Nevertheless, this may change with future studies due to the 
limited sample size for this group (n = 7). There was little difference 
between males and females for high-temperature morbidity and mor-
tality. However, for heatwaves and mortality, females showed a 
noticeably larger increase in mortality compared to males (11.7% (95% 
CI: 7–16.5) vs. 6.6% (95% CI: 2.8–10.5)) respectively. This may reflect 
both physiological and social differences, including reduced thermo-
regulatory capacity due to postmenopausal changes in estrogen levels 
and higher prevalence of living alone, lower income, and chronic health 
conditions among older women, all of which have been associated with 
increased heat vulnerability (van Steen et al., 2019). Heatwave-related 
morbidity could not be analysed due to insufficient data.
Climate change affects populations differently based on location. 
Most of the included studies were conducted in high- and upper-middle- 
income countries, with only two from a low-income country and a few 
from lower-middle-income countries. This imbalance may limit the 
generalisability of the findings to lower-income settings, which are more 
vulnerable to extreme heat due to limited resources and inadequate 
infrastructure, such as air conditioning, home insulation, and healthcare 
access (Bowen and Friel, 2012, Gamble et al., 2013).
The majority of studies in this meta-analysis were conducted in 
Climate Zone C (Mediterranean, Oceanic, Subtropical). Tropical regions 
had the highest morbidity risk from elevated temperatures, while 
Mediterranean climates recorded the highest mortality rates. Heatwave- 
related morbidity was most pronounced in subtropical climates, and 
heatwave-related mortality was highest in tropical climates. These 
findings indicate that mortality and morbidity risks vary across K¨oppen- 
Geiger climate classifications, potentially related to local climatic con-
ditions or factors increasing vulnerability, such as socioeconomic status 
and healthcare infrastructure.
Furthermore, humidity is another key factor, as it exacerbates heat 
stress and may contribute to regional differences in heat-related health 
outcomes (Patel et al., 2019a, Longden, 2019). Sensitivity analyses 
showed virtually identical high-temperature effects on morbidity (no 
humidity adjustment: RR 1.019 [95% CI: 1.008–1.030] vs. adjusted: RR 
1.015 [95% CI: 1.009–1.020]) and mortality (no adjustment: 1.028 
[95% CI: 1.022–1.034] vs. adjusted: 1.021 [95% CI: 1.018–1.024]).
For heatwaves, morbidity risks were modestly higher in studies 
without humidity adjustment (RR 1.247 [95% CI: 1.097–1.418] vs. 
adjusted: 1.107 [95% CI: 1.072–1.144]), while mortality estimates 
remained similar (RR 1.053 [95% CI: 1.025–1.082] vs. adjusted: 1.134 
[95% CI: 1.112–1.157]). These comparisons indicate that humidity 
adjustment or inclusion does not have a large impact on our overall 
temperature–health associations for morbidity and mortality (Appendix 
1; Tables S11-S14). Nevertheless, humidity plays a critical role in 
amplifying physiological heat strain. Epidemiological studies may un-
derestimate its effect due to methodological limitations, limited 
geographical coverage and data constraints, particularly the use of daily 
data and populations with impaired sweating responses. Moreover, at 
high temperatures, humidity may have limited additional influence on 
the health conditions that cause most heat-related deaths (Baldwin et al., 
2023). Thus, the apparent similarity in effect estimates in our synthesis 
may reflect these analytical constraints rather than the absence of a 
physiological effect.
In addition, we explored heatwave intensity and duration gradients 
using subgroup meta-analyses. These analyses showed generally higher 
pooled risks for more intense and longer heatwave events for mortality, 
but effect estimates did not consistently increase across all intensity or 
duration categories, particularly for morbidity outcomes (Appendix 1, 
Tables S13 and S14). Accordingly, the presence of an exposure–response 
gradient was interpreted as supportive of an association, but not as ev-
idence of a strictly monotonic increase in risk. Such patterns may be 
influenced by mortality and morbidity displacement, population adap-
tation, and differences in target effect estimands across studies.
Several factors contribute to the heightened vulnerability of older

adults to heat, including age-related physiological changes, co- 
morbidities and associated medication utilisations, and environmental 
and social factors (Zhang et al., 2016, Ciss´e et al., 2022). Age-related 
impairments in thermoregulation, such as reduced sweat production 
and diminished blood flow to the skin, decrease the body's ability to 
dissipate heat, increasing the likelihood of cardiovascular complica-
tions. Furthermore, the combination of multiple chronic conditions and 
medications exacerbates the risk of adverse health outcomes in response 
to heat (Bunker et al., 2016, Meade et al., 2020). Other crucial factors 
that determine vulnerability are living alone, housing with inadequate 
cooling or heating, behavioural responses to heat stress, and poor access 
to health care (Bunker et al., 2016).
4.4. Uncertainty and heterogeneity in studies
Inter-study heterogeneity was generally high when assessing the 
relationship between morbidity and heat exposures, indicating inherent 
differences between the studies. Some subgroup analyses showed low to 
moderate heterogeneity, while others   such as disease groups, 
healthcare utilisation, study designs, and heatwave intensities   had 
higher heterogeneity. Despite adjustments for meteorological factors 
and air pollutants in most studies, variations in covariates, study pop-
ulations, research methods, and contexts likely contributed to the het-
erogeneity. For mortality and heat exposures, heterogeneity was low to 
moderate. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses indicated consistency in 
the direction and magnitude of the effects, especially for high temper-
atures and mortality, which showed consistently low or no observed 
heterogeneity, indicating high consistency among studies. Factors like 
age structure, access to medical care, social services, housing quality, 
and air conditioning likely further amplified these differences in het-
erogeneity (Bunker et al., 2016). Additionally, morbidity studies can 
have greater variability due to a wider range of health outcomes, 
underreporting, and limited access to care, unlike mortality studies 
which often have more uniform endpoints. Observations that vulnerable 
people often die instead of being hospitalised during heat events may 
explain why heat impacts on morbidity appear smaller and more vari-
able than on mortality (Bunker et al., 2016, Lee et al., 2024, Michelozzi 
et al., 2009, Turner et al., 2012).
Differences in study aims, effect definitions, and analytic choices 
imply that studies may target different underlying effects, making het-
erogeneity in effect estimates expected rather than indicative of low 
strength of evidence. Study-specific covariate structures could not be 
reweighted or standardised across all included studies, which may 
contribute to residual confounding and heterogeneity.
Finally, the absence of upgrading for “large magnitude of effect” 
reflects the conservative Navigation Guide/GRADE criterion, which re-
serves this domain for unusually strong relative effects rather than for 
modest but widespread risks. In the context of heat and heatwaves, 
relative risks well below two can still translate into large pop-
ulation‑attributable burdens because a high proportion of the popula-
tion is exposed. Global assessments have documented substantial and 
increasing burdens of heat‑related mortality and morbidity associated 
with recent human‑induced warming, illustrating how modest relative 
risks at the study level can generate large absolute impacts when 
exposure is ubiquitous (Vicedo-Cabrera et al., 2021, Zhou et al., 2024, 
Ebi et al., 2021, Romanello et al., 2021).
4.5. Strengths and limitations
This review included 623 studies, providing sufficient evidence to 
analyse a wide range of climate zones, income settings, and disease 
outcomes. This enabled detailed stratification by age, sex, climate 
classification, and income group. The limited number of additional 
studies identified through citation screening of existing reviews further 
supports the comprehensiveness of the original search strategy.
This study had several limitations. Only peer-reviewed journal 
articles published in English were included. Significant publication bias 
and high heterogeneity were detected, indicating the possibility of un-
examined factors.
Effect estimates were standardised to reflect per 1◦C increases for 
comparability, but these increments are defined relative to each study’s 
own metric and threshold, and the ranges and interpretations of these 
metrics (for example, dry‑bulb temperature vs composite indices that 
incorporate humidity) could differ across studies.
Consequently, a 1◦C increase in one metric is not physically equiv-
alent to a 1◦C increase in another and our pooled ‘per 1◦C’ estimates 
should be interpreted as standardised statistical contrasts rather than 
strictly comparable physical exposure changes. In addition, stand-
ardisation was based on high‑temperature thresholds defined in the 
original studies, not across the full exposure range, so the pooled esti-
mates do not imply a fully linear exposure–response relationship.
For heat-wave studies, recent methodological work indicates that 
non–heat-wave temperatures can act as confounders and that the choice 
of temperature-adjustment strategy can substantially influence esti-
mated heat-wave effects (Kim and Bell, 2024). In our review, most 
studies estimating heat-wave–health associations did not explicitly 
adjust for non–heat-wave-related temperatures; therefore, the pooled 
heat-wave estimates may plausibly underestimate the total health ef-
fects attributable to heat waves. This highlights the importance of 
careful analytical design and transparent reporting of temperature- 
adjustment strategies in future heat-wave epidemiological studies.
Estimates were also pooled across different disease groups to 
generate overall effects; however, cause-specific subgroup analyses were 
conducted to address variation in disease pathways and reporting. While 
many subgroups included a large number of estimates, others had fewer 
and should be interpreted with caution.
To avoid bias and over-representation of correlated estimates (e.g. 
multiple age groups or lag days within the same study), we summarised 
these using a fixed-effect model to obtain a single study-level estimate. 
This approach assumes that subgroup estimates reflect a common un-
derlying effect within each study and provides an appropriate weighting 
of evidence without over-representing individual studies. While this 
may slightly smooth or attenuate subgroup-specific differences, it yields 
a more stable and interpretable pooled estimate (Riley et al., 2015, 
Borenstein, 2009, Deeks et al., 2024).
Given the large number of included studies, it was not feasible to 
assess every study-specific detail in the risk-of-bias evaluation. A 
consistent approach was applied across all studies, focusing on key do-
mains such as exposure, outcome, and confounding. This ensured 
comparability but may have overlooked finer differences in exposure 
precision, outcome definitions, or covariate adjustment. However, the 
overall assessment captures the best available reflection of study quality 
based on reported information.
Despite including studies from various locations, there were limited 
numbers from low- and middle-income countries, tropical and polar 
regions, the African continent and South America. Studies from Africa 
were largely absent, while those from South America were limited to a 
few locations. These regions may experience greater vulnerability to the 
impacts of a warming climate among older populations, although 
vulnerability factors themselves were not directly assessed in this re-
view. Additionally, most of the original studies lacked data on modifying 
factors such as medication use, pre-existing conditions, and co- 
morbidities. Finally, this review included only four studies investi-
gating heat-driven healthcare costs, limiting the strength of the evidence 
on this association.
5. Conclusion
Our findings underscore the urgent need for climate policies and 
tailored health interventions to mitigate the escalating morbidity and 
mortality risks among older adults. With global climate change expected 
to increase acute heat exposure and the growing vulnerability of an

15 

ageing population, certain regions will face heightened demands on 
social and healthcare services, necessitating innovative policy solutions 
(Falchetta et al., 2024).
The review identified consistent associations between high temper-
atures, heatwaves and multiple heat-sensitive outcomes, with variation 
by exposure definition, outcome type, age group, sex, climate zone and 
national income classification. To better understand the observed vari-
ations across exposures, populations and regions, studies should also 
explore the underlying factors and local resources that influence 
vulnerability and adaptive capacity. This could include individual-level 
data on pre-existing conditions, medication use, heat-sensitive diseases 
and healthcare access. Additional studies are especially needed in un-
derrepresented regions, including the African continent and South 
America, as well as polar and tropical climates, and low- and middle- 
income countries, to understand how health risks vary across 
populations.
Finally, few studies have quantified the healthcare-related economic 
burden of heat, despite its clear challenges on healthcare systems. 
Further cost-focused research is critical to inform policy and support the 
implementation of sustainable and equitable heat mitigation strategies.
