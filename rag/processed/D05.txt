ABSTRACT
Background: Flood disasters are projected to increase in severity, duration, and frequency in the context of 
climate change, and the risks of mortality and morbidity may increase after floods, which will overwhelm health 
systems.
Objective: This study aims to synthesize current epidemiological evidence about the impacts of floods on mor-
tality and morbidity.
Method: After performing a systematic literature search from 2000 to 2023, we included studies involving human 
participants, with exposures of floods, and with outcomes of mortality or morbidity.
Results: In total, 37 studies were included in evidence syntheses. Meta-analyses yielded an overall relative risk of 
1.26 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.10, 1.46), 1.10 (1.08, 1.13), 1.11 (1.04, 1.20), and 1.38 (1.18, 1.62) for all- 
cause mortality and morbidities of overall gastrointestinal diseases, diarrhea diseases, and dysentery, respec-
tively. Although meta-analyses were not conducted, evidence from at least three studies consistently supported 
that exposure to floods was associated with increased risks of malaria and respiratory diseases. The evidence for 
other outcomes was reported but either limited or uncertain.
Conclusion: This study suggests that exposure to floods is associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality 
and morbidities of overall gastrointestinal diseases, diarrhea diseases, dysentery, malaria, and respiratory dis-
eases, while further research is urgently called.
1. Introduction
As the most frequent natural disaster worldwide, floods have dra-
matic and extensive adverse impacts on the environment, economy, and 
human health (Hu et al., 2018a). The number of people affected by 
floods has been increasing in the past four decades (Hu et al., 2018a). As 
estimated, there are 1.81 billion people (23% of the world’s population) 
directly exposed to 1-in-100-year flood disasters (Rentschler et al., 
2022). In the context of climate change, flood disasters are projected to 
increase in severity, duration, and frequency due to more frequent 
extreme precipitation events and rising sea levels (Tellman et al., 2021).
Commonly, official records of health burden arising from floods have 
only considered deaths caused by the direct physical forces (e.g., 
drowning) or hazards (e.g., electrocution and hypothermia) of floods 
(Yan et al., 2020; Lindersson et al., 2023). However, these assessments 
have not considered the substantial number of deaths and diseases 
resulting from non-accidental causes. For example, non-accidental 
deaths and diseases may result from contamination of food and drink-
ing water, exposure to pathogens (i.e., fungi, bacteria, and viruses), 
impaired access to health services, and displacement (Alderman et al., 
2012; Dosa et al., 2020; Paterson et al., 2018). This suggests that the 
health impacts of floods are under-studied. Comprehensively under-
standing the impact of floods on mortality and morbidity is important 
for improving policies, including disaster response strategies and health 
system adaptation.
Of the studies that have examined the relationship between flood 
exposure with mortality and morbidity, the key gaps identified include 
the following (Zhong et al., 2018). First, most of the original studies 
focused on mental disorders, and the findings for other outcomes were 
mixed (Zhong et al., 2018). To our knowledge, no systematic review nor

mapping reviews on this association, which primarily described the 
research pattern and landscape in the field through systematic literature 
searches but without findings synthesis and study quality assessment 
(Alderman et al., 2012; Zhong et al., 2018). In addition, one of them only 
included studies published during 2004–2011 (Alderman et al., 2012), 
while the other one did not consider short-term health impacts (i.e., <6 
months), but included studies published over a more recent and broader 
time period (i.e., before 2016) (Zhong et al., 2018). Therefore, we aimed 
to undertake a systematic review with meta-analyses to address the 
gaps. Specifically, to examine the impacts of floods on mortality and 
morbidity.
2. Methods
This systematic review was reported in accordance with the PRISMA 
statement and was registered on PROSPERO (CRD4202339981). (Page 
et al., 2021).
2.1. Eligibility criteria
For this systematic review, studies were eligible if they were studies 
including humans, with exposures included floods, and with outcomes 
included mortality or morbidity. We excluded studies that only included 
life quality, external cause of deaths (e.g., injury and suicide), symptoms 
(e.g., cough, runny nose, rash, sore throat), or psychological health (e.g., 
post-traumatic stress disorder) as outcomes; and studies focusing on 
disasters that may lead to floods (e.g., hurricanes and earthquakes). We 
excluded studies based on the type of report including reviews, meta- 
analyses, descriptive studies, response letters, and conference ab-
stracts. Studies in all languages were considered for inclusion. We only 
included eligible studies published from 2000 January 1st to 2023 
March 10th. When studies with the same outcomes, same flood events, 
and similar time frames had overlapping populations, only the study 
with the largest population was included.
2.2. Search strategy
OVID Medline, EMBASE, and Scopus were searched to identify 
eligible studies. We did not filter the searches by language. The search 
strategy consisted of search keyword searches and corresponding sub-
ject headings (if available) for flood and mortality or morbidity. The 
search strategies are available in Section 1 of Appendix. The references 
cited in relevant reviews identified in the literature search and publi-
cations linked to conference abstracts were also examined. Authors were 
contacted for additional information.
2.3. Screening and selection
Two researchers (ZY and WH) independently screened the titles and 
abstracts of records identified through the literature search using the 
PICO Portal (https://www.picoportal.org/). Only records that were 
excluded by both researchers were excluded at this stage. Full-text of 
potentially eligible studies were retrieved, and screened independently 
by two researchers (ZY and WH). Disagreements on eligibility were 
resolved by consulting a senior researcher (PY).
2.4. Data extraction
Data extraction was initially piloted by three researchers (ZY, WH, 
and PY) on a sample of four studies to ensure consistency of data 
extraction and to modify the data extraction where required. Following 
piloting, two researchers (ZY and WH) independently performed the 
data extraction. From each study, we extracted author names, publica-
tion year, outcomes, outcome ascertainment method, exposure 
assessment method, control group, source of the study population, flood 
events, study type, time frame of impacts (e.g., infectious morbidity was 
assumed to change during 0–14 days post exposure to flood), statistical 
analysis methods (e.g., adjustment for lag and covariates), effect esti-
mates (and their confidence intervals). In studies where effects were 
reported for multiple time frames (e.g., 0–7 days and 0–14 days post 
flood exposure), the estimate of the longest time frame (i.e., 0–14 days) 
was extracted. Discrepancies and uncertainty were resolved in regular 
meetings of the three researchers (ZY, WH, and PY).
2.5. Assessment of risk of bias
Two researchers (ZY and WH) independently assessed the risk of bias 
(RoB) of each study using the National Institutes of Environmental 
Health Sciences National Toxicology Program Office of Health Assess-
ment and Translation (OHAT) tool (Rooney et al., 2014). As recom-
mended by developers of the OHAT tool, we tailored all criteria for our 
topic (see Table S1). Using these criteria, key domains (i.e., exposure 
assessment, outcome assessment, and confounding bias) and other do-
mains (selection bias, attrition/exclusion bias, and other sources of bias) 
were judged as being at a definitely low, probably low, probably high, or 
definitely high RoB. Studies were judged to be at a “definitely high RoB” 
when they had at least one key domain with definitely high RoB and at 
least four domains with a “probably high” or “definitely high” RoB. 
Studies judged to have a “definitely high” RoB were excluded from any 
synthesis (qualitative or quantitative). Discrepancies and uncertainty 
were resolved in regular meetings of the three researchers (ZY, WH, and 
PY).
2.6. Data syntheses
To determine whether a conclusion can be reached regarding the 
association of flood exposure with mortality and morbidity (Russo, 
2007), inverse variance-weighted random-effects meta-analysis was 
used to combine relative risk (RR) estimates when three or more RRs 
with comparable time frames were available for a particular outcome 
(Wilker et al., 2023). We combined RR estimates based on two time 
frames, namely, <6 months and ≥6 months (Zhong et al., 2018). Esti-
mates representing a single day (e.g. 11th day post exposure to the 
flood) or not measured by RRs were not included in the meta-analysis, 
but are reported. Odds ratios approximate RRs for rare outcomes (i.e. 
with event rates <10%) and so were treated as RRs and included in the 
meta-analyses in this circumstance (Sedgwick, 2014; Grimes and Schulz, 
2008). We chose the random-effects model because we expected there 
would be diversity in population, setting and study design across studies 
(Borenstein et al., 2010). We used the restricted maximum likelihood 
estimator for between study variability and the Knapp-Hartung method 
for confidence intervals (Wilker et al., 2023; Borenstein et al., 2010). We 
quantified inconsistency using the I2 statistic and formally tested for 
heterogeneity using Cochran’s Q test. We used forest plots to visually 
display the effect estimates from studies included in meta-analyses and 
for those not included in a meta-analysis (i.e., when there fewer than 
three estimates for a particular outcome and timeframe). We undertook 
post-hoc sensitivity analyses to examine the impact on the meta-analysis 
estimates of excluding studies with at least three domains with proba-
bly/definitely high RoB and by using fixed-effect models.
3. Results
3.1. Studies characteristics
Of the initially identified 5871 records, 1914 were duplicates, and 
3859 were excluded at the title/abstract screening stage (Fig. 1). From 
the 98 full-text articles retrieved, 50 studies met the inclusion criteria 
(Table S2); however, 13 were judged to be at a definitely high RoB, and 
were excluded from the qualitative and quantitative syntheses

(Table S2). Consequently, a total of 37 studies were synthesized (Fig. 1). 
These studies represented 17 countries/territories in eight sub-regions. 
Most (26/37) studies were conducted in Asia. More than half (20/37) 
of the studies only covered flood events within one year (Fig. 2B). 
Almost all studies were restricted to the impacts within one state/ 
province (Table 1), except for five estimating the impacts of flood events 
across Ireland (Boudou et al., 2021), mainland China (Guo et al., 2020), 
Thailand (Suwanpakdee et al., 2015), Vietnam (Phung et al., 2021), and 
the United Kingdom (Milojevic et al., 2011), respectively. Time-series 
study was the most widely used study design (Fig. 2C).
3.1.1. Outcome ascertainments
A variety of morbidities were assessed as well as all-cause, cardio-
vascular (CVD), and respiratory mortality. Except for two studies 
ascertaining outcomes with standardized tools (Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 
2011, 2016), and three through self-report (Mulder et al., 2019; Milo-
jevic et al., 2012; Pradhan et al., 2007), almost all (32/37) studies were 
based on administrative datasets (Table 1). Among the studies 
employing an administrative dataset, some (9/32) only covered a part of 
the general population because the datasets were from a simple source 
(e.g., datasets of hospital visits, emergency visits, or admissions); how-
ever, most (23/32) were based on a comprehensive source (e.g., diseases 
surveillance network, whole-population insurance database, or mortal-
ity registration) and so comprehensively captured the incidences of 
outcomes in the general population.
3.1.2. Exposure assessments
The exposure assessment method varied importantly across studies 
(Table 1). Almost all studies defined the exposed population as those 
residing in flooded areas during flooded periods. On the temporal scale, 
exposure to floods was defined as days, weeks, months, seasons, years, 
or pregnancy periods, with the flood events based on disaster records 
(Table 1). Using disaster records or interviews, exposure to floods was 
defined on a spatial scale as individuals residing in households 
(Milojevic et al., 2012; Miyamori et al., 2023; Yoshida et al., 2021; 
Vollaard et al., 2004), communities/village/district/town (Boudou 
et al., 2021; Suwanpakdee et al., 2015; Milojevic et al., 2011; Rodri-
guez-Llanes et al., 2011, 2016; Pradhan et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2021a; 
Elsanousi et al., 2018; Vanasse et al., 2016; Deng et al., 2022), cities 
(Yan et al., 2020; Hetherington et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2016, 2019, 2020; 
Zhang et al., 2016a, 2016b, 2019; Hu et al., 2018b; Huang et al., 2016; 
Wade et al., 2014; Ni et al., 2014; Lan et al., 2022), or provinces with 
flood events (Guo et al., 2020; Phung et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2021; Liu 
et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2011). The data used for these 
assessments were from sources including official records, insurance 
certifications, and surveys/interviews. Almost all studies in mainland 
China determined flooded areas and periods by referring to the records 
provided by Yearbooks of Meteorological Disasters, where flood periods 
were defined as days with cumulative rainfall of >80 mm for three 
consecutive days (Ma et al., 2021). Studies in Japan defined flooded 
households as those destroyed or whose primary family caregiver was 
seriously injured or lost jobs due to flood events, which were certified by 
a government-run insurance system (Miyamori et al., 2023; Yoshida 
et al., 2021). A study in Canada ascertained flooded areas using water 
level data from governmental hydraulic records (Vanasse et al., 2016). 
Flooded households were identified through interviews in three studies 
(Mulder et al., 2019; Milojevic et al., 2012; Vollaard et al., 2004). Some 
studies used a distinctive definition of flood exposure. A study in Uganda 
defined flooded villages as those containing or were along flooding 
rivers (Boyce et al., 2016); a study in the USA defined exposure group as 
the population residing in postcode areas where at least one-third of the 
area was flooded (Quist et al., 2022); a study in Cambodia quantified 
exposure as square kilometers of floods in the district of residence 
(Saulnier et al., 2018).
3.1.3. Risk of bias
We judged exposure assessment and confounding as the main risks of 
bias (Table S2), with 13 and 18 included studies judged as at a probably 
or definitely high RoB for these domains respectively. Potential bias in 
the exposure assessments arose from the time frames used (weeks/ 
months/seasons/years), which could have resulted in misclassifying the 
periods before flood events as exposed periods. Potential bias from 
confounding arose from inadequate control of confounders in the 
analysis.
3.2. Associations with mortality
Three studies investigated the risk of all-cause mortality (i.e., deaths 
from all causes) related to floods in the <6 month time interval (Fig. 3). 
(Yan et al., 2020; Milojevic et al., 2012; Pradhan et al., 2007) The 
meta-analysis of the three studies yielded an overall RR of 1.26 (1.10, 
1.46), where the time frames of the estimates varied from four days to 24 
weeks, with some inconsistency (I2 = 30%, p-value of test for hetero-
geneity = 0.24). Only one study in the UK examined the effect of flood in 
the ≥6 month time interval, finding that the risk of all-cause mortality 
decreased in one year (RR 0.90 [0.82, 0.99]) (Milojevic et al., 2011). For 
specific mortality outcomes (Fig. 4), the risk of CVD mortality was found 
to increase during 0–4 days post floods (Yan et al., 2020). However, no 
significant association was found for respiratory mortality (Yan et al., 
2020).
3.3. Associations with morbidity
The non-external hospitalization risk was found increased (RR 1.07 
[1.03, 1.10]) during the four weeks post floods in Vietnam (Phung et al., 
2021). Another study focused on the impacts of a flood event in Japan 
(2018) on population aged >45 years. The authors observed that the risk 
of nursing home admission was three times higher among those affected 
by floods compared with those not affected by floods (Fig. 4). (Miyamori 
et al., 2023) This finding suggested that older people might be more 
vulnerable to the health impacts of floods.

Sixteen studies estimated the impacts of floods on the risks of 
gastrointestinal diseases. In addition to overall gastrointestinal diseases,

the studies examined dysentery, diarrhea, acute gastroenteritis, ver-

Three studies estimated the risk of overall gastrointestinal diseases in 
the <6 month time interval (specifically within three weeks) following

during 1998–2008 in Taiwan (Huang et al., 2016), respectively. The 
meta-analysis yielded an overall RR of 1.10 (1.08, 1.13), with consistent 
RRs (I2 = 0%, p-value of test for heterogeneity = 0.55).
Four studies estimated the impact on diarrhea diseases incidence in 
the <6 month time interval (Milojevic et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2019; Lan 
et al., 2022). The meta-analysis of the effect estimates yielded an overall 
RR of 1.11 (1.04, 1.20), with no evidence of between-study heteroge-
neity (I2 = 0%, p-value of test for heterogeneity = 0.47). Among the 
studies not included in the meta-analysis, the study in Cambodia re-
ported that the risk of diarrhea increased in the current month and third 
month post flood periods (Saulnier et al., 2018), and the study in China 
showed that the risk increased on 11th days post the flood event in 2007 
(Gao et al., 2016). Only a study in China estimated the effect of flood in 
the ≥6 month time interval and found that the risk of diarrhea increased 
after a flood event in 2016 (Zhang et al., 2019).
Seven studies estimated the effects on dysentery in the <6 month 
time interval (Liu et al., 2016, 2017, 2019; Zhang et al., 2016b; Ni et al., 
2014; Ma et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2016). Nine effect estimates were 
extracted because two study provided effect estimates for two locations 
and two flood events, respectively (Liu et al., 2016, 2017). The 
meta-analysis yielded an overall RR of 1.38 (1.18, 1.62). Large 
between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 87%, p-value <0.01); 
however, all the RRs indicated flood events were associated with an 
increased risk of dysentery.
Only one study investigated the impact on acute gastroenteritis risk 
(Mulder et al., 2019). This study found an increased risk of acute 
gastroenteritis (RR 4.20 [2.10, 8.40]) two weeks post a flood event in the 
Netherlands. On the other hand, the risks of enteritis caused by VTEC 
(RR 1.35 [1.25, 1.46]) and cryptosporidiosis (RR 1.57 [1.27, 1.48]) were 
found to increase a year post a flood event in 2015 summer in Ireland 
(Boudou et al., 2021).
3.3.2. Infectious diseases
Ten studies (Fig. 4) investigated the impacts on the risks of infectious 
diseases. Studies found the hospitalization risk of infectious diseases 
increased (RR 1.15 [1.03, 1.29]) during 0–4 weeks post floods in Viet-
nam (Phung et al., 2021), and the hospitalization risk of 
food/water-borne infectious diseases increased during 0–14 days post 
floods (Deng et al., 2022).

3.3.3. Respiratory diseases
Five studies provided evidence for respiratory diseases from Viet-
nam, Netherland, Cambodia, Bangladesh, and the USA, consistently 
suggesting the risk of respiratory diseases would increase after floods, 
although the outcomes varied among all respiratory diseases, acute and 
chronic respiratory diseases, and respiratory infection (Fig. 4). (Phung 
et al., 2021; Mulder et al., 2019; Milojevic et al., 2012; Deng et al., 2022; 
Saulnier et al., 2018)
3.3.4. Cardiovascular diseases
One study in the USA indicated that the hospitalization risk of CVD 
increased (RR 1.01 [1.00, 1.03]) during 0–14 days post floods (Deng 
et al., 2022). However, no significant association was found between 
flood and the risk of acute CVD (1.25 [0.81, 1.92]) (Vanasse et al., 
2016).
3.3.5. Skin and eye diseases
Three studies explored the associations between flood and skin dis-
eases (Fig. 4). (Chen et al., 2021a; Huang et al., 2016; Saulnier et al., 
2018) One study estimated the risk of overall skin diseases (Huang et al., 
2016), while the other studies estimated the risks of skin infection and 
atopic dermatitis. All studies found an increased risk of skin diseases 
post flood events. The time frames varied from a week to three months 
post floods.
The associations for eye diseases were estimated in two studies. One 
study in Taiwan found an increased risk of overall eye diseases during 
0–10 days post flood exposure (Huang et al., 2016). Another study in 
mainland China found a twofold increased risk of acute hemorrhagic 
conjunctivitis in 0–10 days post floods (Liu et al., 2020).
3.3.6. Adverse birth and pregnancy outcomes
The associations of floods with adverse birth outcomes were esti-
mated in two studies (Fig. 4). One study in Canada estimated the asso-
ciations in the year of a flood event (Hetherington et al., 2021), and the 
study in the USA estimated the association during 0–4 years post a flood 
event (Tong et al., 2011). The associations for preterm birth and small 
for gestational age were estimated by both studies. In the two studies, 
the risk of preterm birth was found to increase (RR 1.09 [1.03, 1.16]) 
during 0–4 years post the flood event in the USA, while the other as-
sociations were statistically insignificant. Additionally, the risk of low 
birth weight was also found to increase (RR 1.11 [1.03, 1.21]) during 
0–4 years post the flood event in the USA.
3.3.7. Malnutrition
The risk of malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months was 
estimated by Rodriguez-Llanes et al. in two studies in a district in Orissa, 
India (Fig. 4). (Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 2011, 2016) These studies were 
based on the same population and flood events, but with different time 
frames and sample sizes. They found that the risks of stunting and being 
underweight were higher in exposed group in the second month post 
exposure; however the association for wasting was statistically insig-
nificant (Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 2011). At one year after the flood 
event, they found that there was an increased risk of being underweight 
and wasting (Rodriguez-Llanes et al., 2016).
3.3.8. Cognitive dysfunction
Two studies investigated the impacts of floods on cognitive 
dysfunction (Fig. 4). One estimated the impact on the risk of cognitive 
decline among elderly population (i.e., >65 year-old) during 0–6 
months post a flood event in Japan (Yoshida et al., 2021), and found an 
increased risk among the exposed group (RR 1.12 [1.00, 1.26]). The 
other study assessed the impact of the 1998 Yangtze River Flood on the 
risk of cognitive impairment among 108175 four to eight year-old 
children across 31 provinces in mainland China (Guo et al., 2020). 
This study found an increase risk among children that were exposed to 
the flood event during perinatal period.
4. Discussion
This is the first systematic review for the associations of flood with 
mortality and morbidity. We synthesized findings from previous studies 
after study quality assessment. We found that flood exposure was 
associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality and morbidities of 
overall gastrointestinal diseases, diarrhea diseases, dysentery, malaria, 
and respiratory disease after flood exposure. Evidence for other health 
outcomes was limited or uncertain.
4.1. Health impact pathways
Floods can result in diseases and deaths in several potential path-
ways. First, floods can contaminate water supply systems (Barbetta 
et al., 2022), through which the risks of gastrointestinal diseases can 
increase, and the transmission of some infectious diseases (e.g., typhoid 
and paratyphoid) can be facilitated (Lee et al., 2020). Second, floods can 
create breeding grounds for fungi, bacteria, viruses, and vectors (e.g., 
mice, cockroaches, and mosquitoes) (Suhr and Steinert, 2022). When 
this occurs, outbreaks of infectious diseases, including plague, malaria, 
Japanese encephalitis, and leptospirosis may be triggered (Suhr and 
Steinert, 2022). Third, flood events can force people to evacuate, leading 
to displacement. Even though temporary accommodation may be pro-
vided, hygiene issues still commonly occur from poor access to sanita-
tion facilities (Paterson et al., 2018). Hence, the risks of water-borne 
diseases, infectious diseases, and infections can rise. Displacements 
also mean limited access to medical and health facilities, which may lead 
to delayed diagnoses and treatments, more complications, exacerbated 
prognosis, and consequently elevated mortality; lacking protection from 
environmental hazards (e.g., non-optimal temperatures), which impact 
a broad range of health conditions (Zhao et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2021b; 
Wu et al., 2022); and high densities of fungi, bacteria, virus, and vectors 
in limited rooms (Lee et al., 2020). Forth, mortality and morbidity can 
be exacerbated by disaster-related psychological disorders via many 
mechanisms including weakening immune systems, sleep problems, 
substance abuses, and poor self-care (Trief et al., 2006; Sharpe and 
Davison, 2021).
4.2. Research gaps and future perspectives
4.2.1. Potential effect modifications
Considering the health impact pathways of floods, the health effects 
of flood may be modified by location- (e.g., socioeconomic status, di-
etary habit, sewage system, disaster response strategies, medicine 
sources), climate- (e.g., climate zone, temperature, and humidity), 
biology- (e.g., density of mosquitos, vegetation types), and flood-related 
(e.g., severity, duration, and flood types) factors, as well as vulnerabil-
ities of populations (e.g., children, elderly population, and people with 
pre-existing chronic diseases). Understanding these effect modifications 
helps identify the most vulnerable populations, which is essential for 
targeting protections and preventions. Furthermore, the understanding 
can facilitate predicting the health effects in places where health data 
are not available, mostly low-income countries, which could be used to 
estimate flood-attributable health burden to optimize resource 
allocations.
4.2.2. Temporal and spatial coverages
We found that the temporal (i.e., flood events) and spatial coverages 
(i.e., locations) of almost studies were limited. This means that the 
generalizations of these effect estimates were compromised. To obtain 
more reliable estimates, studies with a broad temporal and spatial 
coverages are warranted.

4.2.3. Time frame and lag effects
Most studies only arbitrarily/empirically assumed a time frame for 
the lag effect, without examining the time frames statistically. Lag ef-
fects for gastrointestinal diseases and infectious diseases have been 
statistically examined by some studies, considering the breeding, incu-
bation, and latent periods of pathogens (Liu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 
2016a; Wang et al., 2021). The findings consistently suggested that the 
risk of gastrointestinal diseases reached the peak on several days post 
flood exposure (Liu et al., 2016, 2017, 2019; Zhang et al., 2016b; Ni 
et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2021). The impacts on mortality and morbidity 
may be delayed because the pathways between flood and human health 
are not immediate. However, the lag effects were understudied for most 
health impacts. Identifying the lag-response associations related to flood 
exposure is a key to coping with elevated mortality and morbidity risks 
after flood events.
4.3. Clinical and policy implications
Both health service providers and policymakers should be aware of 
the increased health risks post floods, when health/medicine service 
demands will sharply rise. Considering that the capability and accessi-
bility of health systems are generally disrupted during floods, the 
unfulfillment for the increasing health/medicine service demands will 
be exacerbated, especially in communities poor in health/medicine re-
sources or with infrastructures vulnerable to floods (Romanello et al., 
2023). To optimize prognosis, reduce avoidable deaths from natural 
causes, and ease flood-attributable health burden, health service pro-
viders should strengthen capacities in medical supplies, human resource 
management, and triage strategy; and policymakers should periodize 
enhancing health system resilience to natural disasters, as well as 
improving disaster preparedness, early warning and detection systems, 
and efficient disaster response protocols to reduce the hazardous expo-
sure during and post floods.
4.4. Conclusions
Previous studies had examined an extensive range of mortality and 
morbidity risks related to floods, although studies were scarce in almost 
all countries/territories. We undertook a systematic review examining 
the associations between floods and health outcomes and found asso-
ciations of flood exposure with elevated risks of all-cause mortality and 
morbidities of overall gastrointestinal diseases, diarrhea diseases, dys-
entery, malaria, and respiratory diseases. These findings suggest that 
medical practitioners and policymakers should raise awareness and 
preparation for the elevated burden on health systems after flood events. 
The evidence for the morbidities of other infectious diseases, cardio-
vascular diseases, skin and eye diseases, adverse birth and pregnancy 
outcomes, malnutrition, and cognitive dysfunction was either limited or 
uncertain. Future studies examining more flood events and locations, 
effect modifiers, and the lag effects of flood exposure with mortality and 
morbidity are needed.
